Reading Passage 3

Cambridge IELTS 20 — Test 3 — Reading Passage 3

Robots and Us · Matching Features + Matching Sentence Endings + Multiple Choice (Q27–40)

📋 Passage Overview

Three specialists — the cosmologist Martin Rees, the engineer Daniel Wolpert and the anthropologist Kathleen Richardson — answer the same four questions about robots: should we use them to colonise other planets, when will machines outthink us, should we be frightened of artificial intelligence, and what can science fiction teach us? The passage is a panel of opinions, not an argument, so the whole task is keeping three voices apart.

What the passage argues: Nothing single — it deliberately sets three positions side by side. Rees is the cautious futurist who sees real moral risk; Wolpert is the practical engineer who thinks the benefits will outweigh the harm and that human-level creativity is still decades away; Richardson is the anthropologist who argues the problem lies in us, in our habit of seeing ourselves in the machines we build.

段落结构 Structure: 文章按“四个问题 × 三位专家”排列。问题一（机器人殖民其他行星）：Rees——小行星采矿比“地球化改造”更现实温和，被殖民行星应享有类似南极的地位；Wolpert——除非能把资源带回地球，否则不必殖民，机器人在地球上用处更大；Richardson——“殖民”在道德上可疑。问题二（机器智能何时超越人类）：Rees——下棋已赢人，但感知环境仍不如小孩，未来将引发道德问题；Wolpert——某些方面已超越，但创造性智能50年内难以实现；Richardson——恐惧源于人类把物体拟人化。问题三（是否该害怕人工智能）：Rees——担心“奇点”与计算机网络形成自主“大脑”；Wolpert——病毒的危害来自恶意设计者，收益仍大于损害；Richardson——机器从未真正挑战人类，许多人欢迎机器智能。问题四（科幻的意义）：Rees——一流科幻胜过二流科学；Wolpert——科幻预测力惊人，几乎没有无机器人的未来；Richardson——现实与虚构的界线是人为划出的，应重新整合。

Task type: Q27–33 Matching Features (match each statement to expert A Rees, B Wolpert or C Richardson; letters may be used more than once), Q34–36 Matching Sentence Endings (choose the correct ending A–D; one ending is never used) and Q37–40 Multiple Choice (four options, one answer).

💡 Reading strategy tip: This is an opinion-attribution passage, and almost every mark depends on who said something rather than what was said. Before you look at Q27, draw a 3×4 grid: three experts down the side, four questions across the top, and write two or three words in each cell as you read. Then Q27–33 (matching features) becomes a lookup, not a search — and remember the instruction NB You may use any letter more than once: Rees answers three of them, Richardson two, Wolpert two. For Q34–36 you need the topic two experts share, so read the endings A–D first. Q37–40 then run in passage order through the last three paragraphs.
⚠️ Traps in this section 本节陷阱:
  • Three experts, one topic — every one of them speaks about colonisation, about machine intelligence and about fear. Finding the topic is never enough; you must find the person whose wording matches the statement. Always check all three answers to a question before you commit.
  • Q28 vs Q33 — both belong to Richardson, and students who assume ‘one letter per statement’ force one of them onto Wolpert. The rubric says NB You may use any letter more than once: A appears three times, C twice, B twice.
  • Q29 ‘many decades’ — only Wolpert attaches a time span to creative intelligence (‘within the next 50 years would be highly ambitious’). Rees also talks about ‘later this century’, but about sensing the environment, not about imagination.
  • Q34 asks about ethics, not about space — ending A (‘robots to explore outer space’) is the obvious-looking choice because all three discuss space. But Rees and Richardson agree specifically on the ethical problem of changing or colonising planets, which is ending C.
  • Q36 is a disagreement, not an agreement — Wolpert says AI has already caused damage (computer viruses); Richardson says nothing has ever risen up against us. Read the stem verb: ‘disagrees’.
  • Q38 ‘a mind of its own’ — Rees mentions several worries. The one the question wants is computer networks behaving ‘like a single brain with a mind of its own’ = artificial intelligence developing independent thought. Option B (abilities humans do not have) is something Rees accepts calmly, not a cause for concern.
  • Q40 ‘What is Richardson doing?’ — this is a function question, not a content question. She is not warning or explaining; she is recommending: ‘we need to bring these different understandings together’.
  • Footnotes are free vocabulary — terraforming and singularity are both defined at the bottom of the passage. Students who skip the asterisks lose time guessing.

🔍 Complex Sentence Analysis

Academic Reading sentences are long because they stack clauses, not because the words are hard. Find the main verb first, then peel off the extra layers. 先找主句谓语动词，再逐层剥离修饰成分。
“The next step would be mining of asteroids, enabling fabrication of large structures in space without having to bring all the raw materials from Earth.”
Main ClauseThe next step would be mining of asteroids.
Subordinate / Relative‘enabling fabrication…’ = a present-participle clause of result; ‘without having to bring…’ = a prepositional phrase with a gerund.
Grammar Notewould here is prediction, not past tense. The -ing clause explains the consequence of the main idea, and without having to + verb means ‘and you would not need to’.
Simple MeaningAfter mapping the solar system, robots could mine asteroids, so big structures could be built in space without shipping materials up from Earth.
中文下一步将是开采小行星，从而可以在太空中制造大型结构，而不必把所有原材料从地球运上去。
“Robots are still limited in their ability to sense their environment: they can’t yet recognise and move the pieces on a real chessboard as cleverly as a child can.”
Main ClauseRobots are still limited in their ability to sense their environment.
Subordinate / RelativeThe colon introduces an example that proves the main statement; ‘as cleverly as a child can’ = a clause of comparison.
Grammar NoteA colon in academic writing means ‘here is the evidence for what I just said’. Note still and yet: both mark a limit that has not gone away.
Simple MeaningRobots can beat us at chess but cannot pick up the pieces as well as a small child can.
中文机器人感知环境的能力仍然有限：它们还无法像小孩那样灵巧地识别并移动真实棋盘上的棋子。
“Richardson believes that our fear of machines becoming too advanced has more to do with human nature than anything intrinsic to the machines themselves.”
Main ClauseRichardson believes that our fear… has more to do with human nature.
Subordinate / Relative‘of machines becoming too advanced’ = a prepositional phrase with a gerund subject; ‘than anything intrinsic to the machines themselves’ = the second half of a comparison.
Grammar Notehave more to do with X than Y is a ranking structure: X is the real cause, Y is not. This single sentence is the whole answer to Q37.
Simple MeaningRichardson thinks our fear of clever machines says more about us than about the machines.
中文理查森认为，我们对机器变得过于先进的恐惧，更多源于人性本身，而非机器固有的任何特质。
“I think we should ensure that robots remain as no more than ‘idiot savants’ lacking the capacity to outwit us, even though they may greatly surpass us in the ability to calculate and process information.”
Main ClauseWe should ensure that robots remain as no more than ‘idiot savants’.
Subordinate / Relative‘lacking the capacity to outwit us’ = a reduced relative clause (= which lack…); ‘even though they may greatly surpass us…’ = a concession clause.
Grammar Noteno more than sets a ceiling, and even though concedes what machines can do. Rees is not denying machine power; he wants a limit on it — which is exactly Q27.
Simple MeaningRees wants robots to stay very good at narrow tasks but never clever enough to outsmart people, even if they calculate far faster than we do.
中文我认为我们应确保机器人始终不过是“白痴天才”，不具备智胜我们的能力，尽管它们在计算和处理信息方面可能远超我们。
“Critically, the benefits of computers outweigh the damage that computer viruses cause.”
Main ClauseThe benefits of computers outweigh the damage.
Subordinate / Relative‘that computer viruses cause’ = a defining relative clause identifying the damage.
Grammar NoteThe sentence adverb Critically means ‘this is the decisive point’, not ‘in a critical way’. Compare Interestingly, Similarly, Unfortunately — all used the same way in this passage.
Simple MeaningComputers do far more good than viruses do harm.
中文关键在于，计算机带来的益处大于计算机病毒造成的损害。
“She then explained that the division did not always exist, and that scientists and technologists made this separation because they wanted to carve out the sphere of their work.”
Main ClauseShe then explained that the division did not always exist and that… they made this separation.
Subordinate / RelativeTwo that-clauses share the verb ‘explained’; ‘because they wanted to carve out…’ = a reason clause.
Grammar NoteRepeating that before the second clause keeps a long sentence clear. Notice this is reported speech — the passage constantly alternates between quoting the experts and reporting them, and both count as their opinions.
Simple MeaningShe said the split between fact and fiction is not natural: scientists created it to mark out their own territory.
中文她接着解释说，这种划分并非一直存在，科学家和技术人员之所以作出这种区分，是因为他们想为自己的工作划出专属领域。

🧠 Reading Logic Training

IELTS Reading tests whether you follow the relationship between ideas: cause, contrast, example, definition, sequence and the writer’s own attitude. 抓住句子之间的逻辑关系，比逐词翻译更重要。

Cause & effect — Richardson’s self-fulfilling loop

Her argument is a chain: we build machines in our own image → we assume they think as we do → we see them as an autonomous threat → ‘we tend to imagine they are greater and more powerful than they really are and subsequently they become so’. That last clause is the sting: the fear helps create the thing feared. Track the arrows and Q37 answers itself.

Contrast — agreement and disagreement between the same three people

Q34–36 exist because the experts sometimes converge and sometimes clash. Rees and Richardson agree that changing other planets is ethically wrong (benign / analogous to Antarctica vs morally dubious). Rees and Wolpert agree on how far machine intelligence has actually got. Wolpert and Richardson disagree about whether AI has already done harm. Same panel, three different pairings.

Comparison — measuring machines against people

The comparisons are everywhere and each one is limited: robots beat humans at chess but cannot move the pieces ‘as cleverly as a child can’; machines navigate and search with an ability that ‘far outstrips humans’ but cannot see or hear ‘with the reliability and flexibility of humans’. Notice the pattern: superior at calculation, inferior at perception. That is the shared view behind Q35.

Examples — the concrete case that carries the argument

Wolpert does not argue about AI danger in the abstract; he gives one example, the computer virus, and then generalises from it: ‘Similarly, while there may be misuses of robotics in the near future, the benefits… are likely to outweigh these negative aspects.’ The word Similarly tells you the example was a model for a wider claim.

Definitions — use the footnotes and the quotation marks

Terraforming and singularity are both defined in footnotes, and ‘idiot savants’ is explained by the words around it (‘lacking the capacity to outwit us, even though they may greatly surpass us in the ability to calculate’). Quotation marks in this passage often signal a term the writer expects you not to know — the explanation is always nearby.

Problem → response — three different prescriptions

Same problem, three answers. Rees: build a ceiling into the technology (‘ensure that robots remain as no more than idiot savants’). Wolpert: accept the trade-off, because benefits outweigh harm. Richardson: change how we think, and ‘bring these different understandings together’. Q40 tests whether you noticed that the third one is a recommendation.

Structure & sequence — four questions, always in the same order of speakers

Rees → Wolpert → Richardson, four times over. Once you see the pattern you can predict where any expert’s view on any topic sits, which turns Q27–33 from a search into a lookup. Marking ‘Q1’–‘Q4’ in the margin beside each block of three is worth two minutes later.

The writer’s attitude — and where hedging hides the answers

The writer is invisible here; the opinions belong to the three experts, and the writer only reports them. Watch the hedges inside their answers: ‘may relate’, ‘would be highly ambitious’, ‘seems unlikely’, ‘are likely to outweigh’. Q29 depends entirely on one of these hedges, so never treat a cautious prediction as a flat statement.

🎯 Question-Type Skill Practice

Skill 1 — Matching Features (Q27–33): who said what

A = Martin Rees, B = Daniel Wolpert, C = Kathleen Richardson. Letters may be used more than once. Decide first, then read the evidence.

  • 27. For our own safety, humans will need to restrict the abilities of robots. → A — ‘we should ensure that robots remain as no more than “idiot savants” lacking the capacity to outwit us’.
  • 28. The risk of robots harming us is less serious than humans believe. → C — ‘we tend to imagine they are greater and more powerful than they really are’; ‘none have in fact risen up and challenged human supremacy’.
  • 29. It will take many decades for robot intelligence to be as imaginative as ours. → B — ‘Expecting a machine close to the creative intelligence of a human within the next 50 years would be highly ambitious.’
  • 30. We may have to start considering whether we are treating robots fairly. → A — ‘Should we feel guilty about exploiting [sophisticated robots]? Should we fret if they are underemployed, frustrated, or bored?’
  • 31. Robots are probably of more help to us on Earth than in space. → B — ‘Using robots to gather resources nearer to home would seem to be a better use of our robotic tools.’
  • 32. High-quality science fiction may be as accurate as mediocre science. → A — ‘better to read first-rate science fiction than second-rate science — more stimulating, and perhaps no more likely to be wrong’.
  • 33. There are those who look forward to robots developing greater intelligence. → C — ‘Not all fear [robots]; many people welcome machine intelligence.’

Habit to build: the statements are not in passage order in a matching-features task. Work expert by expert instead: read everything Rees says, tick off the statements that fit, then move on.

Skill 2 — Matching Sentence Endings (Q34–36): find the shared topic

Each stem names two experts and a relationship (similar views / share an opinion / disagrees). You are matching topics, so read the four endings before you go back to the text.

  • 34. Richardson and Rees express similar views regarding the ethical aspect of… → C — changes made to other planets for our own benefit. Rees calls terraforming less ‘benign’ and wants planets protected like Antarctica; Richardson calls colonisation ‘morally dubious’. Both are talking about ethics, which rules out ending A.
  • 35. Rees and Wolpert share an opinion about the extent of… → B — advances made in machine intelligence so far. Rees: machines win at chess but cannot move the pieces. Wolpert: ‘in a limited sense it already has’, but no machine sees or hears reliably. Same verdict — impressive but narrow.
  • 36. Wolpert disagrees with Richardson on the question of… → D — the harm already done by artificial intelligence. Wolpert: ‘we have already seen the damaging effects of artificial intelligence in the form of computer viruses’. Richardson: ‘none have in fact risen up and challenged human supremacy’.
  • Never used: A — robots to explore outer space. All three discuss space, but no pair is described as agreeing or disagreeing about exploration itself.

Check before you move on: the completed sentence must be grammatical. Read ‘the extent of advances made in machine intelligence so far’ aloud — it fits. ‘The extent of robots to explore outer space’ does not.

Skill 3 — Multiple Choice (Q37–40): answer the question that was asked

These four run in passage order through Richardson’s and Rees’s later answers. Two of them ask about content, two about what the speaker is doing.

  • 37. Richardson on fear of machines → B. ‘it stems from humans’ tendency to personify inanimate objects’ = attributing human characteristics to non-human entities. A is wrong because she links fear to human nature, not to how advanced robots have become.
  • 38. Rees’s cause for concern → C. ‘computer networks… could behave like a single “brain” with a mind of its own, and with goals that may be contrary to human welfare’ = independent thought. B is wrong: he openly accepts that machines may ‘greatly surpass us in the ability to calculate’ without worrying about it.
  • 39. Wolpert on science fiction → B. His final sentence is the emphasis: ‘Interestingly, almost no science fiction envisages a future without robots’ — robots are fundamental to the genre. C describes the ‘spectrum’ sentence, but that is about variety, not change over time.
  • 40. Richardson on reality and fantasy → C. ‘we need to bring these different understandings together to get a whole perspective’ = recommending a change of approach. D is a true detail (scientists made the separation) but it is not what she is doing overall.

Technique for ‘What is X doing?’ questions: ignore the topic and look at the verbs — we need to, perhaps then, should all point to a recommendation; because, this is why point to an explanation.

Skill 4 — Building the grid before you answer

The single highest-value habit for this passage. Three experts × four questions = twelve cells. Fill them in as you skim, in two or three words each.

  • Colonising planets — Rees: map by robots, mine asteroids, protect like Antarctica. Wolpert: no pressing need, use robots on Earth. Richardson: morally dubious, meet ‘the Other’.
  • Machine vs human intelligence — Rees: wins at chess, poor at sensing; moral questions later. Wolpert: already ahead in search and memory, 50 years from creativity. Richardson: the fear is about us, not them.
  • Should we be scared? — Rees: singularity, networks with a mind of their own. Wolpert: viruses come from malicious designers; benefits outweigh harm. Richardson: nothing has ever challenged us; many people welcome AI.
  • Science fiction — Rees: first-rate fiction beats second-rate science. Wolpert: remarkable at predicting; no future without robots. Richardson: the fact/fiction divide is artificial and should be closed.

Tip: with this grid finished, Q27–33 takes about three minutes and you never re-read a paragraph twice.

Skill 5 — Yes / No / Not Given thinking (extra practice)

This passage has no Y/N/NG task, but opinion passages are where the skill matters most. Decide, then check.

  • Rees expects robotic craft to have mapped the solar system by 2100. → YES — ‘the solar system would be mapped by robotic craft by the end of the century’.
  • Wolpert believes colonising other planets is immoral. → NOT GIVEN — his objection is practical (‘no pressing need’), not ethical. The moral objection is Richardson’s.
  • Rees thinks robots may one day deserve moral consideration. → YES — ‘Should we feel guilty about exploiting them? Should we fret if they are… bored?’
  • Wolpert thinks computer viruses show that machines have become intelligent. → NO — ‘the real intelligence is the malicious designer’.
  • Richardson claims that robots will never become dangerous. → NO — she says it ‘seems unlikely’, a hedge, and she also warns that imagining machines as powerful helps make them so.
  • Science fiction writers have influenced real robot design. → NOT GIVEN — Wolpert says fiction predicts the future well, which is not the same as shaping it.

Skill 6 — Summary completion practice (extra)

Cover the passage and read this summary of the three positions. Every bold word is a single word taken straight from the text — exactly what a summary task would ask for.

Rees believes robotic craft will soon map the solar system and that mining asteroids would allow structures to be built in space; he calls this more benign than terraforming and wants colonised planets given a status analogous to Antarctica. Wolpert sees no pressing need to leave Earth, most of which is still inaccessible, and thinks the benefits of technology outweigh its harms. Richardson argues that fear stems from our tendency to personify inanimate objects, notes that no machine has challenged human supremacy, and recommends bringing reality and fantasy back together to gain a whole perspective.

36
D
