Reading Passage 1

Cambridge IELTS 20 — Test 3 — Reading Passage 1

Frozen Food · Notes Completion + True/False/Not Given (Q1–13)

📋 Passage Overview

This passage tells the story of frozen food from a US point of view. It moves in strict chronological order: ancient ice storage and the freeze-drying of potatoes in the Andes, natural ice traded by Boston ship owners, the Australian invention of ‘mechanical ice’ in 1870, Clarence Birdseye’s quick-freezing innovations after 1917, the wartime tin shortage, the spread of home refrigerators in the 1950s, the launch of the ‘TV Dinner’ in 1954, and finally the size of the industry today. Because the text is a timeline, dates and decades are your fastest way to find any answer.

What the passage argues: That frozen food became an everyday product only when several things came together: a way of freezing quickly enough not to damage the food, packaging and transport that shops could afford, a wartime shortage that made canned food scarce, and finally a freezer in the average family’s kitchen. The writer reports rather than argues — the tone is factual throughout.

段落结构 Structure: 全文按时间顺序展开：① 远古用冰保存食物、安第斯山区的冻干土豆；② 19世纪的天然冰贸易（波士顿船东、1851年冷藏车运黄油）；③ 1870年澳大利亚发明"机械制冰"及其原理；④ 1880年冷冻牛羊肉运往英国，但冰晶破坏细胞；⑤ 1912年伯宰（Birdseye）在拉布拉多受因纽特人启发；⑥ 他的三大创新：速冻、包装内冷冻、玻璃纸透明包装；⑦ 20世纪30年代的障碍与租赁方案；⑧ 二战锡短缺带来转机、50年代冰箱普及；⑨ 1954年"电视晚餐"大获成功；⑩ 今日产业规模。

Task type: Two tasks. Questions 1–7: notes completion, ONE WORD ONLY from the passage — you must copy the writer’s exact word. Questions 8–13: TRUE / FALSE / NOT GIVEN about information in the passage (not about the writer’s opinion, so never write YES or NO).

💡 Reading strategy tip: Both tasks here are in passage order, and the notes for Q1–7 even print the year and the country above each gap (2,000 years ago, South America … 1851, USA … 1880, Australia). Use those headings as an index: find the paragraph with that date first, then read only those three or four lines. For the True/False/Not Given set, do the same — every statement contains a name or a date (Boston, Australia, Labrador, Swanson) that pins it to one paragraph. Then ask the only question that matters: does the passage state this, contradict it, or never mention it? Your own knowledge about frozen food is not evidence.
⚠️ Traps in this section 本节陷阱:
  • Q1 needs the food, not the process. The notes say ‘conserved the nutritional value of (1)… using a method of freezing then drying’. The method is already described, so the gap is the thing being preserved: potatoes.
  • Q3 ‘Two kinds of…’ — the passage names beef and mutton. The single word that covers both, and that the passage actually uses, is meat (‘they could export meat across the oceans’). Writing ‘beef and mutton’ breaks the ONE WORD rule.
  • Q6 tin, not cans. The shortage was of the metal: ‘tin was being used for munitions… canned foods were rationed to save tin’. ‘Cans’ is the product, not the shortage.
  • Q8 wealth is never mentioned. The passage says Boston ship owners had ice towed across the Atlantic, but says nothing about how much money they made — a textbook NOT GIVEN.
  • Q10 reverses cause and sequence. Birdseye went to Labrador ‘to trap and trade furs’ after running out of money; the Inuit fascinated him only once he was there. Purpose ≠ result, so the answer is FALSE.
  • Q12 ‘new style of container’. The tray was ‘the same segmented aluminum tray that was used by airlines’ — borrowed, not new. FALSE. Compare Q13, where the industry is never compared with other countries: NOT GIVEN.

🔍 Complex Sentence Analysis

Academic Reading sentences are long because they stack clauses, not because the words are hard. Find the main verb first, then peel off the extra layers. 先找主句谓语动词，再逐层剥离修饰成分。
“In the early 1800s, ship owners from Boston, USA, had enormous blocks of Arctic ice towed all over the Atlantic for the purpose of food preservation.”
Main ClauseShip owners had enormous blocks of Arctic ice towed all over the Atlantic.
Subordinate / Relative‘from Boston, USA’ = a reduced relative phrase identifying the owners; ‘for the purpose of food preservation’ = a purpose phrase.
Grammar Notehave something done (causative): the owners did not tow the ice themselves — they arranged for it to be towed. Note also ‘for the purpose of + noun/-ing’, a formal way of saying ‘in order to’.
Simple MeaningBoston ship owners paid for huge blocks of Arctic ice to be pulled across the Atlantic so that food could be kept fresh.
中文19世纪初，美国波士顿的船东雇人将巨大的北极冰块拖运至大西洋各地，用于保存食物。
“During the freezing process, crystals formed within the cells of the food, and when the ice expanded and the cells burst, this spoilt the flavor and texture of the food.”
Main ClauseCrystals formed within the cells… / this spoilt the flavor and texture of the food.
Subordinate / Relative‘when the ice expanded and the cells burst’ = a time clause with two verbs sharing one ‘when’.
Grammar NoteA four-step causal chain packed into one sentence: form → expand → burst → spoil. The pronoun this refers back to the whole time clause, not to one noun.
Simple MeaningIce crystals grew inside the food; as they got bigger the cells broke, and the food ended up tasting and feeling worse.
中文在冷冻过程中，食物细胞内会形成冰晶；冰晶膨胀使细胞破裂，从而破坏了食物的风味与口感。
“While he was there, he became fascinated with how the Inuit would quickly freeze fish in the Arctic air.”
Main ClauseHe became fascinated with how the Inuit would quickly freeze fish.
Subordinate / Relative‘While he was there’ = a time clause; ‘how the Inuit would…’ = a noun clause acting as the object of ‘fascinated with’.
Grammar Notewould here is not a conditional — it describes a repeated past habit (= used to). ‘While he was there’ also proves the interest came after he arrived, which is the whole point of Question 10.
Simple MeaningOnce he was in Labrador, he became very interested in the Inuit way of freezing fish fast.
中文他在当地期间，对因纽特人利用北极空气快速冷冻鱼类的方法产生了浓厚兴趣。
“In the 1930s, few grocery stores could afford to buy freezers for a market that wasn’t established yet.”
Main ClauseFew grocery stores could afford to buy freezers.
Subordinate / Relative‘that wasn’t established yet’ = a defining relative clause describing the market.
Grammar Notefew (without ‘a’) is negative: almost none. Compare a few = some. IELTS statements often flip this pair, so read the article carefully.
Simple MeaningIn the 1930s hardly any food shops had enough money to buy freezers, because customers for frozen food did not exist yet.
中文20世纪30年代，几乎没有杂货店买得起冷柜，因为当时冷冻食品市场尚未形成。
“American consumers couldn’t resist the combination of a trusted brand name, a single-serving package and the convenience of a meal that could be ready after only 25 minutes in a hot oven.”
Main ClauseAmerican consumers couldn’t resist the combination.
Subordinate / RelativeThree parallel noun phrases (‘a trusted brand name / a single-serving package / the convenience of a meal’) inside ‘the combination of…’, plus the relative clause ‘that could be ready after only 25 minutes’.
Grammar NoteParallel structure: after ‘the combination of’, every item must be the same kind of phrase. Copy this pattern in Writing Task 2 when you list reasons.
Simple MeaningShoppers loved having all three things at once: a name they knew, a portion for one person, and a meal that took only 25 minutes to heat.
中文值得信赖的品牌、单人份包装，加上只需在热烤箱中加热25分钟即可食用的便利，这样的组合让美国消费者无法抗拒。

🧠 Reading Logic Training

IELTS Reading tests whether you follow the relationship between ideas: cause, contrast, example, definition, sequence and the writer’s own attitude. 抓住句子之间的逻辑关系，比逐词翻译更重要。

Cause & effect — why early frozen meat was disappointing

Follow the chain in one sentence: crystals formed within the cells → the ice expanded → the cells burst → this spoilt the flavor and texture. Birdseye’s solution reverses the chain: quick-freezing ‘reduced the damage that crystals caused’. That single link supplies Q4 (crystals) and makes Q9 TRUE. When a passage builds a chain like this, the answer is usually the first item in it — the root cause.

Contrast — ‘while’ does two different jobs here

“While the food frozen this way was still palatable, there was some deterioration” = although (concession). But “Canned foods were rationed…, while frozen foods were abundant and cheap” = whereas (comparison of two facts). Same word, two meanings — decide which one you are reading before you judge a True/False statement.

Comparison — the missing comparison in Question 13

The final paragraph is full of numbers: $67 billion turnover, $26.6 billion for home consumption, a third of food service sales. But nowhere does the writer compare the US industry with any other country. A statement containing a superlative (‘the largest in the world’, ‘the fastest growing’) is NOT GIVEN unless the passage itself makes that comparison. Numbers on the page are not the same as a ranking.

Examples — specific instances stand for a general category

“A shipment of Australian beef and mutton was sent, frozen, to England”, and two lines earlier the passage says cattlemen realised they could “export meat across the oceans”. The notes ask for ‘Two kinds of (3)’, so you need the category word that covers both examples: meat. Practise moving up and down this ladder — examples ↔ category — because summary and note tasks constantly demand it.

Definition & apposition — free explanations inside commas

“…the use of cellophane, the first transparent material for food packaging, which allowed consumers to see the quality of the product.” The commas hand you a definition, and the relative clause hands you the purpose. That purpose is exactly how the note phrases Q5 (‘so the product was visible’), which is how you know cellophane is the answer even if you have never met the word.

Problem → solution — the shape of the 1930s paragraph

Problem 1: shops could not afford freezers → Solution: he leased them cheap freezer cases. Problem 2: he could not ship nationwide → Solution: he leased insulated railroad cars. Problem 3: home freezers were too small → no solution yet — and that unsolved problem is what the 1950s paragraph finally answers with the refrigerator (Q7). Reading the paragraph as problem–solution tells you where the story must go next.

Chronology — the dates are the index to this passage

10,000 years ago (ice storage) → 2,000 years ago (Andean potatoes, Q1) → early 1800s (Boston ice, Q8) → 1851 (butter by rail, Q2) → 1870 (mechanical ice) → 1880 (frozen meat to England, Q3, Q9) → 1912 (Labrador, Q10) → 1917–29 (Birdseye’s freezers, Q4, Q5) → 1930s (obstacles) → early 1940s (tin, Q6) → 1953 (33 million fridges, Q7) → 1954 (TV Dinner, Q11, Q12) → today (Q13). Mark these dates in the margin while you skim and you will never search twice.

The writer’s attitude — reporting, with one dry joke

The tone is neutral and factual: the writer explains, dates and quantifies, but does not praise or condemn frozen food. The only flicker of personality is “This preserved their nutritional value — if not their aesthetic appeal”, a quiet joke that the freeze-dried potatoes looked terrible. Because the passage reports information rather than argues a case, the task uses TRUE/FALSE/NOT GIVEN, not YES/NO/NOT GIVEN — and you must never mark a statement FALSE simply because the writer sounds unenthusiastic.

🎯 Question-Type Skill Practice

Skill 1 — Notes Completion (Q1–7): use the date headings as an index

ONE WORD ONLY, copied from the passage. Each note block prints a year and a country, so you never need to search the whole text.

  • Q1 potatoes — heading ‘2,000 years ago, South America’. The passage: ‘the inhabitants of South America’s Andean mountains had a unique means of conserving potatoes… This preserved their nutritional value’. The freezing-then-drying method is already in the note, so the gap must be the food.
  • Q2 butter — heading ‘1851, USA’. ‘Railroads first began putting blocks of ice in insulated rail cars to send butter…’. ‘Specially adapted trains’ = ‘insulated rail cars’.
  • Q3 meat — heading ‘1880, Australia’. The two kinds are beef and mutton, but you may write only ONE word, so use the category word the passage itself supplies: ‘they could export meat across the oceans’.
  • Q4 crystals — ‘quick-freezing techniques that reduced the damage that crystals caused’. The note says ‘so that (4) did not spoil the food’; the verb is plural, so keep the -s.
  • Q5 cellophane — ‘the use of cellophane, the first transparent material for food packaging, which allowed consumers to see the quality’ = ‘so the product was visible’.
  • Q6 tin — ‘tin was being used for munitions. Canned foods were rationed to save tin’. The note says ‘a shortage of’, so name the metal, not the cans.
  • Q7 refrigerator — ‘By 1953, 33 million US families owned a refrigerator’. ‘A large number of homes’ = ‘33 million US families’. The note uses ‘a’, so the singular form is required.

Check before you move on: read each completed note aloud. If it is not grammatical English, or if it needs two words, you have taken the wrong word.

Skill 2 — TRUE / FALSE / NOT GIVEN (Q8–13)

Decide for yourself first, then read the reason. Remember: FALSE = the passage says the opposite; NOT GIVEN = the passage simply never says.

  • 8. The ice business made some Boston ship owners very wealthy. → NOT GIVEN — we are told they had ice towed across the Atlantic for food preservation, and nothing at all about their profits. Plausible is not the same as stated.
  • 9. A disadvantage of the Australian process was that it affected the taste of food. → TRUE — ‘there was some deterioration… this spoilt the flavor and texture’. Flavour = taste.
  • 10. Birdseye travelled to Labrador in order to learn how the Inuit froze fish. → FALSE — he ‘ran out of money and went to Labrador to trap and trade furs’; only ‘while he was there’ did he become fascinated by the fish. The statement changes his purpose.
  • 11. Swanson invested a great deal of money in promoting the TV Dinner. → TRUE — ‘with the help of a clever name and a huge advertising budget’. Advertising budget = money spent on promotion.
  • 12. Swanson developed a new style of container for the launch. → FALSE — the meal came ‘in the same segmented aluminum tray that was used by airlines’. Borrowed, therefore not new.
  • 13. The US frozen food industry is currently the largest in the world. → NOT GIVEN — the last paragraph gives US figures only and makes no international comparison.

Habit to build: underline the risky word in each statement — wealthy, taste, in order to, a great deal, new, largest. That word, not the topic, decides the answer.

Skill 3 — Locating information fast

Which paragraph would you scan for each idea? Train yourself to jump to the right block instead of re-reading from the beginning.

  • How ancient peoples freeze-dried food → paragraph 1 (Andes, 2,000 years ago).
  • Ice moved by ship and by rail → paragraph 2 (early 1800s, 1851).
  • How a refrigeration machine actually works → paragraph 3 (compressor, condenser, evaporator) — a ‘no-question zone’: skim it.
  • Why early frozen meat lost quality → paragraph 4 (1880, crystals, cells burst).
  • Where Birdseye got his idea → paragraph 5 (Labrador, Inuit).
  • His three innovations → paragraph 7 (quick-freezing, freezing in the package, cellophane).
  • Why shops and homes could not use frozen food yet → paragraph 8 (the 1930s obstacles).
  • The wartime boost and the spread of home fridges → paragraph 9 (early 1940s, 1953).
  • The TV Dinner story → paragraph 10 (1954, 13 million sold, $2.7 billion by 1959).
  • The size of the industry now → the final paragraph.

Tip: in a chronological passage, pencil the year beside each paragraph while you skim. It is the cheapest index you will ever build, and it works for every question type.

Skill 5 — Summary completion practice (extra)

Skill 6 — Matching dates and people (extra)

A quick memory check on the timeline. Match each date to what happened; then cover the answers and say them aloud.

  • 1851 — ice-filled insulated rail cars carry butter from Ogdensburg to Boston.
  • 1870 — Australian inventors produce ‘mechanical ice’, first used to cool beer.
  • 1880 — frozen Australian beef and mutton reach England.
  • 1912 — Birdseye, out of money, goes to Labrador to trade furs.
  • 1917 — he returns to the USA and starts building quick-freezing machines.
  • 1929 — his business is sold and becomes General Foods; he stays as director of research.
  • 1953 — 33 million US families own a refrigerator.
  • 1954 — Swanson launches the first TV Dinner; 13 million are sold within a year.
  • 1959 — Americans spend $2.7 billion a year on frozen food, half a billion of it on ready meals.

Exam value: in a date-heavy passage, examiners build distractors out of neighbouring years (1912 vs 1917, 1953 vs 1954). Always match the date to the event, never to the nearest number.

His purpose was furs; the interest in freezing came ‘while he was there’. That distinction is exactly why Q10 is FALSE.

This sentence supplies Q6: the shortage was of tin, not of cans or of food generally.

The passage lists exactly three things after ‘the combination of’. Parallel lists like this are favourite sources of multiple-choice options.
