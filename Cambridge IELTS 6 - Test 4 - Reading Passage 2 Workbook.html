Cambridge IELTS 6 — Test 4 — Reading Passage 2

Do Literate Women Make Better Mothers? · Practice Workbook

📋 Passage Overview

This passage reports on a long-term study in Nicaragua that investigated whether teaching mothers to read and write directly improves their children's health and survival, independent of family wealth. It explains how a nationwide literacy campaign let researchers separate the effect of literacy itself from other family factors, and reports a striking drop in infant mortality among newly literate mothers. The passage closes by considering what these findings mean for how governments and aid agencies should invest in women's education.

💡 Reading strategy tip: This passage mixes narrative background with numerical evidence (mortality rates per 1,000 births). Before reading in detail, scan for all numbers and dates first — they are the anchors most completion and multiple-choice questions are built around.

🔍 Complex Sentence Analysis

"We've known for a long time that maternal education is important, but we thought we would have to wait a generation for the pay-off."
Main ClauseWe've known for a long time...but we thought...
Subordinate Clause"that maternal education is important" (noun clause, object of "known"); "we would have to wait a generation for the pay-off" (noun clause, object of "thought")
Grammar NoteTwo parallel noun clauses act as objects of "known" and "thought"; "but" signals contrast between a long-held belief and an assumption now being revised
Simple MeaningResearchers always believed educating mothers mattered, but assumed benefits would only appear in the next generation.
中文我们早就知道母亲的教育很重要，但我们原以为要等到下一代才能看到成效。
"Because education is usually acquired in childhood, it is difficult to know whether healthier children result from literacy itself or from the wealth and attitudes of the families who send their daughters to school."
Main Clauseit is difficult to know whether healthier children result from literacy itself or from the wealth and attitudes...
Subordinate Clause"Because education is usually acquired in childhood" (reason clause); "whether...or..." (embedded question, object of "know"); "who send their daughters to school" (relative clause modifying "families")
Grammar NoteDummy subject "it" + adjective + infinitive ("it is difficult to know"); the embedded "whether...or" question is the object of "know"
Simple MeaningBecause most literate women learned to read as girls, it's hard to tell if literacy or family background causes healthier children.
中文由于识字能力通常是在童年时期获得的，很难判断健康的孩子究竟是识字本身带来的结果，还是那些送女儿上学的家庭的财富和态度所致。
"By 1985, roughly 300,000 adults who had never attended primary school had learned, through Nicaragua's National Literacy Crusade, to read, write and use numbers."
Main Clauseroughly 300,000 adults...had learned...to read, write and use numbers
Subordinate Clause"who had never attended primary school" (relative clause modifying "adults"); "By 1985" (time adverbial)
Grammar NotePast perfect "had learned" shows an action completed before a past reference point ("by 1985"); the relative clause gives essential defining information about the adults
Simple MeaningNearly 300,000 previously unschooled adults became literate through a national campaign by 1985.
中文到1985年，约有30万从未上过小学的成年人通过尼加拉瓜全国扫盲运动学会了读写和算术。
"Although no one is certain why, researchers suspect that newly literate mothers make better use of clinics, have smaller families, or simply gain more confidence in caring for their children."
Main Clauseresearchers suspect that newly literate mothers make better use of clinics, have smaller families, or simply gain more confidence...
Subordinate Clause"Although no one is certain why" (concessive clause); "that newly literate mothers make better use..." (noun clause, object of "suspect")
Grammar Note"Although" concedes uncertainty before presenting a claim; three parallel verb phrases share the same subject inside the noun clause
Simple MeaningExperts don't know for sure why, but think literate mothers may use healthcare better, have fewer children, or feel more capable as parents.
中文尽管没有人能确定原因，但研究人员推测，新识字的母亲可能更善于利用诊所、家庭规模更小，或者只是在照顾孩子方面更有自信。
"There is growing evidence that female education, at any age, is an important health intervention in its own right."
Main ClauseThere is growing evidence
Subordinate Clause"that female education, at any age, is an important health intervention in its own right" (noun clause, in apposition to "evidence")
Grammar NoteThe "that" clause functions as an appositive, restating what the "evidence" actually is; "at any age" is a parenthetical adverbial emphasising the finding applies beyond childhood education
Simple MeaningMore proof is emerging that educating women, no matter their age, directly improves health outcomes.
中文越来越多的证据表明，无论在什么年龄阶段接受教育，女性教育本身就是一项重要的健康干预措施。

🧠 Reading Logic Training

Cause & Effect

Cause: mothers learn to read and write through the Literacy Crusade. → Effect: infant mortality among their children drops from 105 to 84 per 1,000 births.

Contrast

The passage contrasts illiterate mothers' unchanged infant mortality rate (staying near 110) with newly literate mothers' rate, which fell to 84 per 1,000 by 1985 — a 21-point gap.

Comparison

Mothers educated in primary school had a low, stable mortality rate (80/1,000) throughout, while newly literate adult mothers moved from 105 down to 84 — showing adult literacy can approach the benefit of childhood schooling.

Examples

Examples of possible explanations researchers are investigating include: better use of clinics and hospitals, smaller family size, greater control within the household, and increased self-respect.

Definition

A "direct causal effect" is defined in the passage as literacy itself improving child health outcomes, independent of a family's wealth or attitudes.

Problem / Solution

Problem: earlier evidence for literacy's benefit was only correlational, confounded with family wealth. Solution: the Nicaraguan Crusade offered a natural experiment reaching women who had never attended school, letting researchers isolate literacy's true effect.

Chronology

1979 — Nicaraguan government launches social programmes → Literacy Crusade rolls out → by 1985, roughly 300,000 adults become literate → researchers survey nearly 3,000 women → compare mortality rates before and after the campaign.

Author's Attitude

The writer presents the findings with cautious optimism, quoting Cleland's warning that Nicaragua's campaign was "special in many ways" and might not be easily replicated elsewhere — a balanced, not overly confident, tone.
