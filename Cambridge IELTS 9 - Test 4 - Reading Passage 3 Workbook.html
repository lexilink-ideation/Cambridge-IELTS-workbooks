Cambridge IELTS 9 — Test 4 — Reading Passage 3

Practice Workbook

📋 Passage Overview

The development of museums. Six paragraphs (A–F) argue that museums have shifted from scholarly storerooms to visitor 'experiences'. The writer covers the criticism of vulgarisation, the blurring of boundaries between museums, heritage sites and theme parks, the commercial pressure on interpreters of history, how accuracy is bent to match public perceptions, and how the survival of only durable buildings biases our view of the past. Question types: matching headings (Q27–30), multiple choice (Q31–36) and True/False/Not Given (Q37–40).

💡 Reading tip: For matching headings, read the first and last sentences of each paragraph and ask what the paragraph does, not what it mentions. Paragraph D mentions theme parks, but its function is to describe the commercial squeeze on heritage professionals. Watch the last paragraph carefully for Q40: the closing claim about nostalgia and bias is the writer's main point.

🔍 Complex Sentence Analysis

The conviction that historical relics provide infallible testimony about the past is rooted in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, when science was regarded as objective and value free.
Main ClauseThe conviction… is rooted in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries.
Subordinate / Relative'that historical relics provide infallible testimony about the past' (appositive that-clause) + 'when science was regarded as objective and value free' (relative clause of time).
Grammar Note'The conviction that…' names a belief; 'is rooted in' locates its origin. The 'when' clause explains why the belief arose.
Simple MeaningThe belief that historical objects tell the truth about the past comes from an era when science was thought to be objective.
中文认为历史遗物能为过去提供绝对可靠证据的这一信念，根植于 19 世纪和 20 世纪初——当时科学被视为客观且价值中立的。
Museums used to look - and some still do - much like storage rooms of objects packed together in showcases: good for scholars who wanted to study the subtle differences in design, but not for the ordinary visitor, to whom it all looked alike.
Main ClauseMuseums used to look much like storage rooms of objects packed together in showcases.
Subordinate / Relative'- and some still do -' (parenthetical) + the colon introduces the evaluation + 'who wanted to study the subtle differences' and 'to whom it all looked alike' (two relative clauses).
Grammar Note'to whom' is a formal preposition + relative pronoun. This sentence supplies Q31's answer: museums served scholars, not the public.
Simple MeaningMuseums looked like storerooms of crammed showcases — useful for scholars studying fine design differences, but confusing for ordinary visitors.
中文博物馆过去看上去——有些如今仍然如此——很像堆满展柜的储藏室：这对想研究设计细微差异的学者很有用，但对普通参观者而言，一切看起来都一样。
Such developments have been criticised as an intolerable vulgarisation, but the success of many historical theme parks and similar locations suggests that the majority of the public does not share this opinion.
Main ClauseSuch developments have been criticised… but the success… suggests that the majority of the public does not share this opinion.
Subordinate / Relative'that the majority of the public does not share this opinion' = reported clause.
Grammar NoteA balanced 'but' sentence presenting criticism and popular approval side by side — precisely the 'mixed views' of heading ii.
Simple MeaningCritics call these changes unbearably vulgar, but the popularity of heritage theme parks suggests most people disagree.
中文此类发展被批评为难以容忍的庸俗化，但许多历史主题公园及类似场所的成功表明，大多数公众并不认同这一看法。
Those who are professionally engaged in the art of interpreting history are thus in a difficult position, as they must steer a narrow course between the demands of 'evidence' and 'attractiveness', especially given the increasing need in the heritage industry for income-generating activities.
Main ClauseThose… are thus in a difficult position.
Subordinate / Relative'who are professionally engaged in the art of interpreting history' (relative) + 'as they must steer a narrow course…' (reason) + 'especially given the increasing need…' (concessive/causal phrase).
Grammar Note'given' here means 'considering'. This sentence answers Q34 directly: experts must balance conflicting priorities.
Simple MeaningHistory interpreters face a dilemma: they must balance evidence against attractiveness, especially as heritage increasingly needs to make money.
中文因此，以诠释历史为业的人处境艰难：他们必须在「证据」与「吸引力」的要求之间小心权衡，尤其是考虑到遗产行业对创收活动日益增长的需求。
Such presentations tell us more about contemporary perceptions of the world than about our ancestors.
Main ClauseSuch presentations tell us more about contemporary perceptions of the world than about our ancestors.
Subordinate / RelativeNo subordinate clause — a compact comparative.
Grammar Note'more about X than about Y' is the whole point of paragraph E, and the exact wording of Q35's correct option.
Simple MeaningThese displays reveal today's beliefs rather than the truth about our ancestors.
中文这类展示告诉我们的，更多是当代人对世界的看法，而不是我们祖先的真实情况。
Yet in museums, fine period rooms give only an image of the lifestyle of the upper class of that era.
Main ClauseFine period rooms give only an image of the lifestyle of the upper class of that era.
Subordinate / RelativeNo subordinate clause; 'Yet' links back to the crowded reality of ordinary Leyden housing.
Grammar Note'only' carries the argument: the display is partial, not false. This underpins Q40 (museums can give a false impression).
Simple MeaningBut museum period rooms show only how the rich lived at that time.
中文然而在博物馆中，精美的时代陈列室只呈现了那个年代上层阶级的生活方式。

🧠 Reading Logic Training

Then vs now: paragraph A describes the old scholarly museum; paragraph B announces the change ('Recently, however, attitudes… have altered'). Every question about 'museums of the past' points to A; every question about current trends points to B onwards.

Convergence: 'museums have adopted story lines… sites have accepted theming… theme parks are moving towards more authenticity'. Three parallel clauses show movement from both directions towards the middle — the basis of heading vi and of Q33.

Concession and counter-evidence: 'Such developments have been criticised as an intolerable vulgarisation, but the success of many historical theme parks… suggests that the majority of the public does not share this opinion.' Criticism plus popularity equals 'mixed views' (heading ii).

Dilemma structure: paragraph D sets two demands against each other — 'evidence' vs 'attractiveness' — and adds commercial pressure on top. Q34's correct option ('have to balance conflicting priorities') is a direct paraphrase of 'steer a narrow course between'.

Concession within a criticism: 'There is one compensation, however, for the professionals who make these interpretations: if they did not provide the interpretation, visitors would do it for themselves.' The writer criticises expert bias, then defends experts as the lesser evil.

Cause & effect (survival bias): durable buildings survive (cause) → museums display grand period rooms (effect) → visitors feel nostalgia (effect) → they wrongly conclude life was better in the past (effect). Q36 and Q40 sit at opposite ends of this chain.

Author's attitude — analytical, not hostile: the writer accepts that experience-based display is popular and that expert interpretation beats visitor guesswork, but insists throughout that all representation contains bias. He diagnoses rather than condemns.
