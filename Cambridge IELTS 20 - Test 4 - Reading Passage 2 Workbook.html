Reading Passage 2

Cambridge IELTS 20 — Test 4 — Reading Passage 2

Adapting to the Effects of Climate Change · Matching Information + Sentence Completion + Matching People (Q14–26)

📋 Passage Overview

This passage is a tour of five real adaptation projects around the world: raising the roads of Miami Beach, growing mangroves back behind bamboo dams in Java, building floating houses in the Mekong Delta, planting salt-tolerant vegetables in Bangladesh, and cooling the streets of Los Angeles with reflective paint and trees. Paragraph A is the introduction; paragraphs B–F are five separate case studies, each with one named expert who is quoted. Because the structure is ‘one place per paragraph’, almost every question is really asking you which country — find the place and you have found the answer.

What the passage argues: That adaptation is no longer optional — warming is already locked in for decades — and that the most interesting answers are coming from the places hit hardest. It also quietly argues for a second point: single-purpose engineering (a seawall, a taller house) works less well than solutions that work with natural processes and with the local community.

段落结构 Structure: 第A段：总起——即使今天停止排放，变暖仍将持续约40年；受影响最重的地区反而创新最活跃。第B段：美国迈阿密海滩——抬高道路61厘米、安装大功率抽水泵（Pineda）。第C段：印尼爪哇——海堤失效，改用竹制半透水坝帮助红树林自然恢复（Tol）。第D段：越南湄公河三角洲——可漂浮房屋，造价仅为抬高地基的三分之一（English）。第E段：孟加拉国——土壤盐碱化，改种耐盐作物，年收成增加二至三次（Chowdhury）。第F段：美国洛杉矶——GIS定位少树街道，铺浅灰反光路面降温6°C（Spotts）。

Task type: Q14–17 Matching information to paragraphs A–F (not in passage order); Q18–22 Sentence completion — ONE WORD ONLY from the passage, and these do follow the order of the passage; Q23–26 Matching statements to five named people A–E, each person used once.

💡 Reading strategy tip: Build a five-line map before you touch the questions: B = Miami Beach / raised roads + pumps / Pineda; C = Java, Indonesia / mangroves + bamboo dams / Tol; D = Mekong Delta, Vietnam / floating houses / English; E = Bangladesh / salty soil + new crops / Chowdhury; F = Los Angeles / cool pavement + trees / Spotts. That map answers Q14–17 (which paragraph), Q18–22 (the sentences run in paragraph order B→F) and Q23–26 (which person) all at once. For the sentence completion remember ONE WORD ONLY: if you want to write ‘semi-permeable dams’ you must cut it to dams. And note that Q23–26 are not in passage order — matching-people questions almost never are.
⚠️ Traps in this section 本节陷阱:
  • Q15 — two numbers, one meaning. Paragraph A contains ‘40 years’ and paragraph B contains ‘20, 30, 40 years’. Both mention 40 years, so the number cannot decide it. The question asks how long until we stop noticing the effects, which is the scientists’ forecast in A, not the maintenance schedule in B.
  • Q18 word limit. The passage says ‘new pumps that can remove up to 75,000 litres of water per minute’. The answer is pumps alone — ‘new pumps’ or ‘water pumps’ is two words and scores zero. Same trap in Q19 (dams, not ‘semi-permeable dams’) and Q21 (crops, not ‘salt-tolerant crops’).
  • Q20 needs a verb. ‘houses that can ______’ — after can only a bare verb fits. The passage word is float. Students who write ‘floating’ or ‘amphibious’ lose the mark on grammar, not on comprehension.
  • Q22 is about trees, not paint. Paragraph F describes two things: planting trees and a cool-pavement coating. The sentence says ‘increased the number of ______’, and you can only count trees. ‘Pavements’ or ‘coatings’ were not increased in number.
  • Q23 vs Q26 — two very similar-sounding statements. Tol (B) talks about approaches that ‘engage and benefit communities’ = involve and help residents. English (C) talks about the technology being needed ‘in places that didn’t previously need it’ = the number of locations has grown. Both sound community-friendly; only one is about numbers of places.
  • Q25 traps you into paragraph A. ‘More work will need to be done in future decades’ sounds like the 40-year forecast, but the statement is attributed to a person. Only Pineda says ‘we’ll need to go back in there and adjust’ — future work, by the same team.

mimicv.to copy the way something behaves模仿；仿效“…to mimic the role of mangrove roots…”The core idea of the whole paragraph: engineering that copies nature instead of fighting it.

stiltsn. pl.tall posts that hold a building above the ground支柱；高脚桩“Homes in this part of Vietnam are traditionally built on stilts.”The old solution. The new one is to let the house rise and fall with the water instead.

tolerantadj.able to survive difficult conditions耐受的；能忍受的“…cultivating naturally salt-tolerant crops in the region.”Two meanings in this passage: plants that tolerate salt, and farmers who are more tolerant of change. Noun: tolerance.
cultivatev.to grow plants on land种植；耕作“…to start cultivating naturally salt-tolerant crops…”More formal than ‘grow’. Noun cultivation; a person who does it is a cultivator.
barrenadj.producing no plants or crops贫瘠的；不毛的“…comparing a barren plot of land to the ‘beautiful, lush green vegetable garden’…”The opposite pair barren / lush is how the writer shows the result without giving a statistic.

thermaladj.relating to heat热的；热成像的“…the only cool spot on an otherwise red thermal image…”Red = hot on a thermal image. The visual proof that the coating works, seen from the Space Station.

🔍 Complex Sentence Analysis

Academic Reading sentences are long because they stack clauses, not because the words are hard. Find the main verb first, then peel off the extra layers. 先找主句谓语动词，再逐层剥离修饰成分。
“Even if we stopped all CO2 emissions tomorrow, we would continue to see the impact of the CO2 already released since industrial times, with scientists forecasting that global warming would continue for around 40 years.”
Main ClauseWe would continue to see the impact of the CO2 already released since industrial times.
Subordinate / Relative‘Even if we stopped all CO2 emissions tomorrow’ = a concessive conditional clause; ‘with scientists forecasting…’ = a with + participle clause adding evidence.
Grammar NoteEven if + past simple, would + verb is the second conditional used to concede: ‘imagine the best case — it still would not help.’ The with + -ing structure is a compact way to attach an expert opinion without starting a new sentence.
Simple MeaningStopping emissions today would not stop the warming; scientists say it would keep going for about 40 more years.
中文即使我们明天停止所有二氧化碳排放，工业时代以来已经排放的二氧化碳仍会持续产生影响，科学家预测全球变暖还将持续约40年。
“In Miami Beach, Florida, USA, seawater isn’t just breaching the island city’s walls, it’s seeping up through the ground, so the only way to save the city is to lift it up above sea level.”
Main ClauseThree clauses: seawater isn’t just breaching the walls / it’s seeping up through the ground / the only way to save the city is to lift it up.
Subordinate / RelativeThere is no subordinator before the second clause — the writer uses a comma and lets ‘not just…’ do the joining work; ‘so’ then marks the conclusion.
Grammar Notenot just X, (but) Y upgrades the problem: the wall is not the only weak point. Because the water comes from underneath, a taller wall is useless — which is exactly why the city raises the roads instead.
Simple MeaningThe sea does not only come over the walls in Miami Beach; it also rises through the ground, so the city must be lifted.
中文在美国佛罗里达州的迈阿密海滩，海水不仅冲破了这座岛城的堤墙，还从地下渗上来，因此拯救这座城市的唯一办法就是把它抬高到海平面以上。
“So, Wetlands International started out with a different approach, building semi-permeable dams, made from bamboo poles and brushwood, to mimic the role of mangrove roots and create favourable conditions for mangroves to grow back naturally.”
Main ClauseWetlands International started out with a different approach.
Subordinate / Relative‘building semi-permeable dams’ = a present-participle clause explaining how; ‘made from bamboo poles and brushwood’ = a reduced relative clause (= which are made from…); ‘to mimic… and create…’ = two infinitives of purpose sharing one to.
Grammar NoteNotice the three-layer stack: action → description → purpose. Key to Q19: the question says ‘the first stage of a project’ and the passage says ‘started out with… building… dams’.
Simple MeaningWetlands International began differently: they built bamboo dams that act like mangrove roots so the mangroves could return by themselves.
中文因此，湿地国际组织采用了不同的方法：用竹竿和灌木枝搭建半透水坝，模仿红树林根系的作用，为红树林自然恢复创造有利条件。
“Homes in this part of Vietnam are traditionally built on stilts but these ones had been built to float.”
Main ClauseTwo coordinated clauses joined by but: homes are traditionally built on stilts / these ones had been built to float.
Subordinate / Relative‘to float’ = an infinitive of purpose after a passive verb.
Grammar NoteThe tense contrast carries the meaning: present simple (‘are traditionally built’ = the old normal) against past perfect passive (‘had been built’ = done before the flood arrived). Key to Q20: the gap follows ‘houses that can ______’, so you need the bare verb float.
Simple MeaningPeople there normally put houses on tall posts, but these four houses were made to sit on the water instead.
中文越南这一带的房屋传统上都建在高脚桩上，但这几栋房子却是被造成能够漂浮的。
“Certain varieties of carrot, potato, kohlrabi, cabbage and beetroot have been found to be better suited to salty soil than the rice and wheat that is typically grown there.”
Main ClauseCertain varieties of carrot, potato, kohlrabi, cabbage and beetroot have been found to be better suited to salty soil.
Subordinate / Relative‘than the rice and wheat…’ = the second half of the comparison; ‘that is typically grown there’ = a defining relative clause describing rice and wheat.
Grammar Notebe found to be = research has shown that they are. The comparison is between two sets of crops, which is why the answer to Q21 is the general word crops and not the name of any one vegetable.
Simple MeaningSome kinds of carrot, potato, kohlrabi, cabbage and beetroot cope with salty ground better than the usual rice and wheat.
中文研究发现，某些品种的胡萝卜、土豆、球茎甘蓝、卷心菜和甜菜根比当地通常种植的水稻和小麦更适应盐碱土壤。
“Spotts says one of these streets, in the Winnetka neighbourhood of San Fernando Valley, can now be seen as a pale crescent, the only cool spot on an otherwise red thermal image, from the International Space Station.”
Main ClauseOne of these streets can now be seen as a pale crescent from the International Space Station.
Subordinate / Relative‘in the Winnetka neighbourhood of San Fernando Valley’ = a place phrase inside the subject; ‘the only cool spot on an otherwise red thermal image’ = an appositive renaming ‘a pale crescent’.
Grammar NoteThree pieces of information are pushed between the subject and the final phrase, so the sentence looks hard. Strip the commas away and only seven words are left. Otherwise here means ‘in every other respect’ — everything except that one street is red (hot).
Simple MeaningFrom space, that one painted street shows up as a pale curve — the single cool place on a map where everything else is red.
中文斯波茨说，圣费尔南多谷温内特卡社区的其中一条街，如今从国际空间站上看去是一道浅色的弧形——在一片红色热成像图中唯一的凉爽区域。

🧠 Reading Logic Training

IELTS Reading tests whether you follow the relationship between ideas: cause, contrast, example, definition, sequence and the writer’s own attitude. 抓住句子之间的逻辑关系，比逐词翻译更重要。

Cause & effect — why each place chose the solution it did

Every case study follows the same chain: a specific local problem → a specific engineering answer. Miami Beach: the water rises through the ground, so a wall cannot help → raise the roads and pump. Java: the shore is soft and muddy, so seawalls collapse → use flexible bamboo dams instead. Vietnam: taller houses are at greater risk from wind damage → let the house float rather than lifting it. When you can state the local problem in one clause, you can predict the paragraph’s answer.

Contrast — ‘rather than’, ‘instead’, ‘but’

The passage is built on reversals of the obvious. “Homes… are traditionally built on stilts but these ones had been built to float.” “Rather than fighting against it, one project is helping communities adapt to salt-affected soils.” Wetlands International ‘started out with a different approach’. Each of these signals marks the innovation — and the innovation is what the questions are about.

Comparison — the numbers that rank things

Only two real comparisons are made, and both are tested. Cost: floating houses are ‘about a third of what it would cost to completely replace a building’s foundations’ (Q16). Crops: certain vegetables are ‘better suited to salty soil than the rice and wheat that is typically grown there’ (Q21). If a question contains a comparative form, go looking for than, more, less or a fraction in the passage.

Examples — a named case is doing the arguing

The writer never says ‘adaptation works’ in the abstract; five named places do the arguing instead. Notice that the evidence is always a number or an image: 61 centimetres of road, 75,000 litres a minute, a third of the cost, two to three more harvests, 6°C cooler, one pale crescent seen from space. In matching-information tasks, these concrete details are your fastest way back to the right paragraph.

Definition & apposition — free explanations inside commas

“coastal mangroves: ecosystems of trees and shrubs that help defend coastal areas…” — the colon gives you the whole of Q14 without a dictionary. The same trick names every expert: “Susanna Tol of the not-for-profit organisation Wetlands International”, “Raisa Chowdhury of the international development organisation ICCO Cooperation”. When you see a colon or a pair of commas after a technical noun, slow down: the answer is often inside them.

Problem → solution → limitation

Notice that the writer is honest about limits, and the limitation sentence often comes last. Java: the programme ‘has seen moderate success, mainly in areas with less subsidence’. Miami Beach: ‘the job is far from over’. Mangrove planting itself: ‘while hugely popular, the majority… fail’. If a True/False/Not Given task were built on this passage, these hedged sentences would be where the FALSE items came from.

Chronology — time is used sparingly, so it stands out

Only four time references appear: ‘since industrial times’, the 40-year forecast (A), ‘in 20, 30, 40 years’ (B), September 2018 (D) and ‘since 2006’ (D). Because there are so few, each one is worth marking. Q15 turns on separating the forecast in A from the maintenance plan in B — two different 40-year statements in adjacent paragraphs.

The writer’s attitude — positive, but never triumphant

The tone is admiring: ‘innovation is thriving’, ‘ambitious but much-needed’, ‘another plus comes from…’. But every case is qualified — moderate success, far from over, most projects fail. The writer’s real position is the one Tol states: single-solution engineering is not enough, and lasting answers must be multifunctional and community-based. Any answer option claiming these projects have solved the problem would be wrong.

🎯 Question-Type Skill Practice

Skill 1 — Matching Information to Paragraphs (Q14–17)

These questions are not in passage order, so never read from the top. Turn each question into one keyword, then check your five-line map.

  • 14. How a type of plant functions as a natural protection for coastlines → C. The keyword is plant. Only paragraph C has plants defending a coast: mangrove roots trap sediment, raise the sea bed and dampen wave energy. Paragraph F also mentions trees — but for shade, not for coastal defence.
  • 15. A prediction about how long it could take to stop noticing the effects → A. The keyword is prediction = ‘scientists forecasting’. Warming would continue ‘for around 40 years’. Paragraph B also says ‘40 years’, but that is a plan to go back and do more work, not a forecast of when the effects stop.
  • 16. A reference to a solution being particularly cost-effective → D. The keyword is cost: ‘much cheaper… about a third of what it would cost to completely replace a building’s foundations’. No other paragraph mentions money at all — a rare gift in this task type.
  • 17. A technology used to locate areas most in need of intervention → F. The keyword is locate: the Geographic Information System data mapping tool ‘identified streets with low tree canopy cover’. Note that identify is the passage’s word for locate.

Habit to build: in a passage of case studies, matching-information questions are really country questions. Write the country beside each letter before you start and you will answer all four in under three minutes.

Skill 2 — Sentence Completion, ONE WORD ONLY (Q18–22)

Two decisions per gap: (1) what part of speech does the grammar demand? (2) can I say it in one word? Do both before you look at the passage.

  • Q18 pumps — ‘the installation of efficient ______’ needs a plural countable noun that can be installed. Passage: ‘the city has set up new pumps that can remove up to 75,000 litres of water per minute’. ‘Efficient’ is the question’s paraphrase of that capacity.
  • Q19 dams — ‘The construction of ______ was the first stage’ needs a noun you can build. Passage: ‘started out with a different approach, building semi-permeable dams’. ‘Started out with’ = ‘the first stage’.
  • Q20 float — after ‘houses that can’ only a bare infinitive fits. Passage: ‘these ones had been built to float’. Reject floating, amphibious and rise.
  • Q21 crops — ‘various ______ that are suitable for…’ needs a plural noun covering carrots, potatoes, cabbage and beetroot together. Passage: ‘cultivating naturally salt-tolerant crops’. One word means you drop ‘salt-tolerant’.
  • Q22 trees — ‘increased the number of ______’ needs something countable. Passage: the Cool Streets programme includes ‘the planting of trees’. The pavement coating is applied, not counted.

Check before you move on: read each finished sentence aloud. ‘Houses that can floating’ is instantly wrong to the ear — use your ear as the last grammar check.

Skill 3 — Matching Statements to People (Q23–26)

Five names, four statements — so one person is not used. Find the quotation marks first: in this passage every answer is inside a direct quote.

  • 23. Strategies must involve and help residents → B (Tol). ‘It’s critical that we transition towards multifunctional approaches that engage and benefit communities and local decision-makers.’ engage = involve; benefit = help; communities = residents. Three-for-three.
  • 24. Heat reduction is vital for survival here → E (Spotts). ‘Urban cooling is literally a matter of life and death for our future in LA.’ The paraphrase pair is ‘life and death’ = ‘absolutely vital for our survival’.
  • 25. More work will be needed in future decades → A (Pineda). ‘We know that in 20, 30, 40 years, we’ll need to go back in there and adjust to the changing environment.’ ‘20, 30, 40 years’ = ‘future decades’; the whole paragraph is about flooding, which gives you ‘rising water levels’.
  • 26. The number of places needing flood adaptation has grown → C (English). ‘This technology is becoming necessary in places that didn’t previously need it.’ That is a statement about the spread of the problem, not about the technology itself.
  • Not used: D (Chowdhury). His quotes are about salinity figures and the contrast between a barren plot and a lush garden — useful colour, but no statement matches it.

Habit to build: underline every pair of quotation marks in the passage before you read the statements. In people-matching tasks the answer is almost always inside them, and unquoted description is almost always a distractor.

Skill 4 — True / False / Not Given thinking (extra practice)

Same passage, a task type that is not in the real exam paper here. Decide first, then read the reason.

  • Miami Beach has raised every road in the city by 61 centimetres. → FALSE — the work started ‘in the lowest and most vulnerable neighbourhoods’ and roads were raised ‘by as much as’ 61 cm, which states a maximum, not a uniform figure.
  • Most attempts to replant mangroves in Java have been unsuccessful. → TRUE — ‘while hugely popular, the majority of mangrove-planting projects fail’.
  • Floating houses are more expensive than houses built on stilts. → NOT GIVEN — the comparison made is with replacing foundations, never with stilt houses.
  • Salt-tolerant vegetables have allowed some Bangladeshi farmers to harvest more often. → TRUE — ‘increases of two to three more harvests per year’.
  • The cool pavement coating lowered air temperature in LA by 6°C. → FALSE — the 6°C figure is for road surface temperature; the city’s average temperature goal is 1.5°C.

Watch the quantity words: every, most, all, only, as much as. They decide more TRUE/FALSE items than the topic vocabulary does.

Skill 6 — Multiple choice thinking (extra)

MCQ options are built from real passage words in the wrong relationship. Learn to spot the swap.

  • Why can a seawall not save Miami Beach? Because the seawater also rises through the ground, not only over the walls. A tempting wrong option would say ‘because the walls are too low’ — true-sounding, but not what the passage says.
  • What is the purpose of the bamboo dams? To create the conditions for mangroves to return naturally — not to replace mangroves permanently, and not to stop all wave action.
  • Why are amphibious houses preferred to raised houses? Two reasons are given: lower cost and less wind risk. An option offering only one of the two is incomplete but not wrong; an option saying ‘they last longer’ is invented.
  • What does the GIS tool do? It identifies which streets need treating. It does not lower the temperature — the coating does that.

Rule: when two options both appear in the passage, choose the one that answers the question stem, not the one that repeats the most passage words.

Q1
B. Global warming would continue for around 40 years.
Scientists forecast that warming ‘would continue for around 40 years’ because of CO2 already released. This forecast is what makes Q15 paragraph A.
Q2
C. Because seawater also seeps up through the ground.
Water rising from below cannot be blocked by a wall, which is why the city raises roads and installs pumps instead. (The soft ground problem belongs to Java, paragraph C.)
Q3
B. pumps — the instruction says ONE WORD ONLY
‘ONE WORD ONLY’ means adjectives must be dropped. The efficient equipment installed is the pumps that remove up to 75,000 litres per minute.
Q4
B. By trapping sediment, raising the sea bed and reducing wave energy.
This three-part description is exactly what Q14 calls ‘how a type of plant functions as a natural protection for coastlines’, and it is the reason the answer is paragraph C.
Q5
C. To copy mangrove roots so mangroves could return naturally.
The dams ‘mimic the role of mangrove roots and create favourable conditions for mangroves to grow back naturally’. This is also the first stage referred to in Q19 (dams).
Q6
B. Because “houses that can…” must be followed by a bare verb.
After the modal can, English requires the base form. The passage supplies it: ‘these ones had been built to float’.
Q7
A. Lower cost and reduced wind risk.
They cost about a third of replacing foundations, and they avoid ‘the problem of taller houses being at greater risk from wind damage’. The cost point is what makes Q16 paragraph D.
Q8
C. By introducing crops that tolerate salt, raising harvests by two to three a year.
‘Rather than fighting against it’, the project helps farmers adapt. Carrot, potato, kohlrabi, cabbage and beetroot varieties suit salty soil better than rice and wheat — the answer to Q21 is the general word crops.
Q9
B. It identified streets with low tree canopy cover.
Identifying where treatment is needed = ‘a technology used to locate areas most in need of intervention’, which is why Q17 is paragraph F. The 6°C drop comes from the coating, not the tool.
Q10
B. Susanna Tol
Tol criticises ‘single-solution focused’ infrastructure and calls for multifunctional approaches that ‘engage and benefit communities and local decision-makers’ — the answer to Q23.
Q11
C. English — “this technology is becoming necessary in places that didn’t previously need it”
‘Places that didn’t previously need it’ is a statement about the number of locations spreading. Pineda’s quote is about future work (Q25), not about more places.
Q12
B. Adaptation is necessary and inventive, but every project so far has clear limits.
Each case is admired and then qualified: ‘the job is far from over’, ‘the majority of mangrove-planting projects fail’, ‘moderate success… mainly in areas with less subsidence’. Paragraph A also states that warming continues even if emissions stop, which rules out D.
