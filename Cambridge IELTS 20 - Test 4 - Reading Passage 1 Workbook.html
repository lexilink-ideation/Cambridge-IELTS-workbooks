Reading Passage 1

Cambridge IELTS 20 — Test 4 — Reading Passage 1

Georgia O’Keeffe · Note Completion + True / False / Not Given (Q1–13)

📋 Passage Overview

This passage is a biography of the American painter Georgia O’Keeffe (1887–1986). It follows her life in order: a farm childhood in Wisconsin, art training and years as a school teacher, the charcoal drawings that brought her to the attention of the photographer Alfred Stieglitz, fame in New York for her skyscrapers and her giant flowers, and finally four decades of painting the bones, cliffs and landscape of New Mexico. Because it is strictly chronological, the fastest way to find any answer is to find the right decade first.

What the passage argues: That O’Keeffe’s importance comes from her independence: for seventy years she ignored passing art fashions and kept painting the same underlying idea — the essential, abstract shapes hidden inside natural things, whether a flower, a bone, a cliff or a cloud.

段落结构 Structure: 第1段：总述——七十年艺术生涯，不随潮流，从自然中提炼抽象形态。第2段：出身与求学，1911–1918年在多州任美术教师。第3–4段：木炭抽象素描经朋友转交 Stieglitz，1916年在其画廊展出。第5段：1918年赴纽约，Stieglitz 举办22场个展，1924年结婚，1917–37年为她拍摄肖像。第6–7段：1920年代中期成名——摩天大楼与放大的花卉。第8段：1929年首赴新墨西哥，1949年定居。第9段：骨骼题材（牛头骨、骨盆系列）。第10段：真正长期吸引她的是新墨西哥的壮丽风景。第11段：Ghost Ranch 与 Abiquiu 两处住所，庭院黑门系列。第12段：1950–70年代旅行与航拍视角的河流与云天巨幅。第13段：晚年因病与失明创作减少，1986年去世，留下约900幅作品。

Task type: Q1–7 Note completion — ONE WORD ONLY from the passage (writing two words scores zero). Q8–13 True / False / Not Given — statements about facts in the passage, not about the writer’s opinion, so the labels are TRUE/FALSE/NOT GIVEN, never YES/NO.

💡 Reading strategy tip: Two task types sit on one passage and they run in separate passes. The seven notes (Q1–7) follow the passage from beginning to end, so treat them as a timeline: teacher → drawings → New York → flowers → New Mexico → landscape → late aerial paintings. Then start again at the top for True / False / Not Given (Q8–13), which also runs in order. For the notes the instruction is ONE WORD ONLY from the passage, so you may not join two words together (‘cow skull’, ‘abstract drawings’) and you may not invent a synonym — copy the exact word, with the exact plural.
⚠️ Traps in this section 本节陷阱:
  • Q1 needs the job, not the place. The sentence lists Virginia, Texas and South Carolina, and the note already says ‘in various places in the USA’. The gap is the role: she ‘became an art teacher’. Do not write ‘art teacher’ — that is two words.
  • Q2 asks what she drew with. The passage says ‘abstract compositions in charcoal’. ‘Abstract’ and ‘drawings’ are both already in the note, so the only new word left is the material.
  • Q3 vs Q4 — two kinds of subject in the same paragraph. She is ‘widely known for the architectural pictures… soaring skyscrapers’ but ‘most often, she painted botanical subjects’. The word but separates the city answer from the flower answer.
  • Q5 is the material, not the animal. What sparked her imagination in New Mexico was the ‘sun-bleached bones’. ‘Cow skull’ and ‘pelvis’ are the individual paintings that came afterwards, and both would break the one-word rule anyway.
  • Q6 ‘for over forty years’ is the locator. The passage says the landscape ‘held the artist’s imagination for more than four decades’. Four decades = forty years. The word needed is landscape, singular.
  • Q7: clouds are already given. The note says ‘pictures of clouds and (7) seen from above’, and the passage names two series: ‘aerial views of rivers’ and paintings of the sky above clouds. Cross off what the note already uses.
  • Q10 is FALSE, not TRUE, because of one small detail. She sent the drawings to a friend, and the friend showed them to Stieglitz. The statement says she sent them to his gallery. Same event, wrong route.
  • Q12 and Q13 are both NOT GIVEN. The passage tells you how many patio pictures she made and that her last two decades were unproductive, but it never says which works were her favourites and never compares the output of the 1950s–70s with any other period. No opinion, no comparison → NOT GIVEN.

🔍 Complex Sentence Analysis

Academic Reading sentences are long because they stack clauses, not because the words are hard. Find the main verb first, then peel off the extra layers. 先找主句谓语动词，再逐层剥离修饰成分。
“Remarkably, she remained independent from shifting art trends and her work stayed true to her own vision, which was based on finding the essential, abstract forms in nature.”
Main Clauseshe remained independent from shifting art trends and her work stayed true to her own vision — two coordinated main clauses joined by and.
Subordinate / Relativewhich was based on finding the essential, abstract forms in nature — a non-defining relative clause describing her own vision, not the whole sentence.
Grammar Noteremain and stay are both linking verbs here, so they take adjectives (independent, true), not adverbs. The comment adverb Remarkably at the front tells you the writer finds this surprising.
Simple MeaningArt fashions changed around her, but she ignored them and kept painting the abstract shapes she found in nature.
中文值得注意的是，她始终不为艺术潮流所动，作品忠于自己的理念——从自然中提炼出本质的、抽象的形态。
“By the time she graduated from high school in 1905, she had determined to make her way as an artist.”
Main Clauseshe had determined to make her way as an artist.
Subordinate / RelativeBy the time she graduated from high school in 1905 — an adverbial clause of time.
Grammar NoteBy the time + past simple in the clause, past perfect in the main clause. The past perfect proves the decision came first and the graduation second.
Simple MeaningShe had already decided to be an artist before she left school.
中文到1905年高中毕业时，她已经下定决心要成为一名艺术家。
“She sent some of these drawings to a friend in New York, who showed them to art collector and photographer Alfred Stieglitz in January 1916.”
Main ClauseShe sent some of these drawings to a friend in New York.
Subordinate / Relativewho showed them to… Alfred Stieglitz in January 1916 — a non-defining relative clause whose subject who refers back to the friend, not to O’Keeffe.
Grammar NoteThe whole of Q10 turns on the antecedent of who. Two people act in this sentence: O’Keeffe sends, the friend shows. Track pronouns and you keep the chain of events straight.
Simple MeaningShe posted the drawings to a friend, and it was the friend who put them in front of Stieglitz.
中文她把其中一些素描寄给了纽约的一位朋友，这位朋友于1916年1月把它们拿给艺术收藏家兼摄影师 Alfred Stieglitz 看。
“However, it was the region’s spectacular landscape, with its unusual geological formations, vivid colours, clarity of light, and exotic vegetation, that held the artist’s imagination for more than four decades.”
Main Clauseit was the region’s spectacular landscape that held the artist’s imagination — an it… that cleft sentence.
Subordinate / Relativewith its unusual geological formations, vivid colours, clarity of light, and exotic vegetation — a long with-phrase listing four features, dropped in between the subject and the verb.
Grammar NoteThe cleft structure exists to emphasise: not the bones, not the buildings — the landscape. Cross out everything between the commas and the sentence becomes short again.
Simple MeaningWhat really kept her interested for over forty years was the landscape itself, with its strange rocks, bright colours, clear light and unusual plants.
中文然而，真正让这位艺术家着迷四十多年的，是该地区壮丽的风景——奇特的地质构造、浓烈的色彩、澄澈的光线以及奇异的植被。
“When in 1965 she successfully translated one of her cloud motifs to a monumental canvas measuring 6 metres in length, it was an enormous challenge and a special feat for an artist nearing eighty years of age.”
Main Clauseit was an enormous challenge and a special feat for an artist.
Subordinate / RelativeWhen in 1965 she successfully translated one of her cloud motifs to a monumental canvas — a time clause; measuring 6 metres in length and nearing eighty years of age are reduced relative clauses (= which measured… / who was nearing…).
Grammar NoteTwo -ing reduced clauses in one sentence. Expanding them back out (which measured, who was nearing) is the quickest way to unpack a dense Academic Reading sentence.
Simple MeaningIn 1965, when she was almost eighty, she painted one of her cloud pictures on a six-metre canvas — a very hard thing to do.
中文1965年，她成功地把一幅云的母题移到一块长达6米的巨幅画布上；对一位年近八十的艺术家来说，这既是巨大的挑战，也是非凡的成就。
“The last two decades of the artist’s life were relatively unproductive as ill health and blindness hindered her ability to work.”
Main ClauseThe last two decades… were relatively unproductive.
Subordinate / Relativeas ill health and blindness hindered her ability to work — a reason clause introduced by as (= because).
Grammar Noteas has three common jobs in Academic Reading: because, while, and in the role of. Here it is because. Note also the hedging adverb relatively.
Simple MeaningShe produced much less in her final twenty years because she was ill and losing her sight.
中文这位艺术家生命的最后二十年产量相对较少，因为疾病与失明削弱了她的创作能力。

🧠 Reading Logic Training

IELTS Reading tests whether you follow the relationship between ideas: cause, contrast, example, definition, sequence and the writer’s own attitude. 抓住句子之间的逻辑关系，比逐词翻译更重要。

Cause & effect — the chain that made her famous

Charcoal drawings → sent to a friend → friend shows Stieglitz → Stieglitz exhibits them → Stieglitz offers “encouragement and promise of financial support” → she moves to New York in 1918 → twenty-two solo exhibitions. Notice how many links there are. Question 10 fails on the second link only: the drawings went to a friend, not to the gallery. In cause-and-effect chains, True/False items are almost always built by removing or swapping one step.

Contrast — what ‘But’ and ‘However’ are doing

Two contrast words carry two answers in this passage. “…widely known for the architectural pictures… But most often, she painted botanical subjects” splits Q3 (skyscrapers) from Q4 (flowers). Later, “However, it was the region’s spectacular landscape…” moves you off the bones (Q5) and onto the landscape (Q6). Train yourself to slow down, not speed up, at a contrast word.

Comparison — the trap in Question 13

The passage says the last two decades were “relatively unproductive”. That is a comparison, but it is a comparison between her late years and her earlier years in general. Question 13 asks something much more specific: was the output of the 1950s–70s greater than in any other period? The passage never ranks the periods, so the answer is NOT GIVEN. Whenever a statement contains more than, the most, or than at any other time, look for the passage to make that exact comparison — and if it does not, stop.

Examples — the general word and the specific word

The passage repeatedly gives you a category and then an example: “botanical subjects” → flowers; “architectural pictures” → skyscrapers; “rocks, cliffs, and mountains” → the landscape they belong to. For a one-word note gap, decide which level the note needs. Q3 and Q4 want the specific noun; Q6 wants the general one, because the note itself says “various features that together formed…”.

Definition & apposition — free information inside commas and dashes

“art collector and photographer Alfred Stieglitz” tells you who he is before you ever meet the name. “O’Keeffe eventually owned two homes in New Mexico — the first, her summer retreat at Ghost Ranch… while the second, used as her winter residence, was in… Abiquiu”: the dash opens a two-part definition, and the first / the second keep the two houses apart. Reading the punctuation is faster than reading the words.

Problem → response — how she met physical limits

Problem: a cloud picture six metres long is beyond one pair of hands. Response: “with the help of assistants”. Problem: ill health and blindness. Response: none — the output simply falls. The writer reports both without drama, which is why an option saying she ‘overcame’ her illness would be wrong.

Chronology — dates are your fastest index

1887 (born) → 1905 (high school; Art Institute of Chicago) → 1907–8 (Art Students League) → 1911–18 (teaching — Q1) → Jan 1916 (Stieglitz sees the drawings) → 1918 (moves to New York) → 1917–37 (the portraits — Q11) → 1924 (marriage; first magnified flowers — Q4) → mid-1920s (skyscraper fame — Q3) → 1929 (first New Mexico trip) → 1946–60 (patio pictures — Q12) → 1949 (moves permanently) → 1950s–70s (travel; aerial series — Q7) → 1965 (6-metre canvas) → 1986 (dies, aged 98). Write the decade beside each paragraph as you skim and the whole passage becomes searchable.

The writer’s attitude — admiring, but reporting facts

The praise is real but it is carried by single words, not by opinions: Remarkably, exceptionally keen, great finesse, exquisitely, a special feat, rich legacy. Everything else is dated fact. That is exactly why the task here is TRUE/FALSE/NOT GIVEN (facts) rather than YES/NO/NOT GIVEN (claims) — and why the two statements about her favourites and her most productive period have to be NOT GIVEN.

🎯 Question-Type Skill Practice

Skill 1 — Note Completion (Q1–7): obey the word limit

ONE WORD ONLY from the passage. Every answer must be copied exactly, in the form the note needs.

  • Q1 teacher — ‘she became an art teacher and taught in elementary schools, high schools, and colleges in Virginia, Texas, and South Carolina’. The note already supplies ‘in various places in the USA’, so the gap is the job. ‘Art teacher’ = two words = 0 marks.
  • Q2 charcoal — ‘abstract compositions in charcoal’. The note says ‘created drawings using ___’, so the gap is a material. Uncountable, no plural.
  • Q3 skyscrapers — ‘architectural pictures that dramatically depict the soaring skyscrapers of New York’. The note says ‘the city’s ___’, so keep the plural.
  • Q4 flowers — ‘In her magnified images depicting flowers, begun in 1924’. ‘Magnified’ = ‘close-up’. Not ‘botanical subjects’, which is two words.
  • Q5 bones — ‘it was the numerous sun-bleached bones… that sparked her imagination’. ‘Sparked her imagination’ = ‘was initially inspired’.
  • Q6 landscape — ‘it was the region’s spectacular landscape… that held the artist’s imagination for more than four decades’. Singular.
  • Q7 rivers — ‘aerial views of rivers and expansive paintings of the sky viewed from just above clouds’. The note already names the clouds, so take the other series.

Check before you move on: read the completed note aloud. If it is not grammatical English, or if it needs two words, your answer is wrong.

Skill 2 — TRUE / FALSE / NOT GIVEN (Q8–13)

FALSE = the passage says the opposite. NOT GIVEN = the passage never says. Decide first, then read the reason.

  • 8. Her style was greatly influenced by changing art fashions. → FALSE — ‘she remained independent from shifting art trends’. Independent from = not influenced by. A direct contradiction.
  • 9. She had already decided on her career when she left school. → TRUE — ‘By the time she graduated from high school in 1905, she had determined to make her way as an artist.’ The past perfect does the work.
  • 10. Stieglitz found her work because she sent drawings to his gallery. → FALSE — she sent them ‘to a friend in New York, who showed them to … Stieglitz’. The route is wrong, so the statement is contradicted, not merely absent.
  • 11. She was the subject of his photographs for many years. → TRUE — ‘Stieglitz’s celebrated black-and-white portraits of O’Keeffe, taken over the course of twenty years (1917–37)’. Twenty years = many years.
  • 12. The patio pictures were among her favourite works. → NOT GIVEN — we are told the patio was ‘particularly inspirational’ and that she made more than thirty pictures of it, but never that she ranked them highest. Inspiration is not preference.
  • 13. She produced more work in the 1950s–70s than at any other time. → NOT GIVEN — the passage says only that her last two decades were relatively unproductive. It never compares the quantity produced in different periods.

Habit to build: underline the risky word in each statement — greatly, already, his gallery, many years, favourite, than at any other time. That word, not the topic, decides the answer.

Skill 3 — Locating information fast

Which paragraph would you scan for each idea? Train yourself to jump to the right block instead of re-reading from the top.

  • Her independence from art fashions → paragraph 1 (the summary paragraph).
  • Where she trained and where she taught → paragraph 2 (1905–1918).
  • How Stieglitz first saw her work → paragraph 3 (the friend, January 1916).
  • The photographic portraits → paragraph 5 (1917–37, marriage 1924).
  • Skyscrapers and giant flowers → paragraphs 6–7 (mid-1920s).
  • Cow skulls and the Pelvis series → paragraph 9 (New Mexico, bones).
  • The two houses and the black door → paragraph 11 (Ghost Ranch and Abiquiu).
  • Painting from planes → paragraph 12 (1950s–70s).
  • Her death and her total output → the final paragraph (1986, some 900 paintings).

Tip: in a biographical passage, one paragraph usually equals one phase of the life. Number the paragraphs with decades before you read a single question.

Skill 4 — Matching headings practice (extra)

This passage does not use headings in the exam, but practising them is the best test of whether you grasped each paragraph as a whole. Headings summarise a paragraph, never one detail.

  • Paragraph 2 → Training, and years of earning a living — not ‘A childhood on a farm’, which is only the first sentence.
  • Paragraph 3 → A new direction, and a lucky introduction.
  • Paragraph 6 → Fame from two very different subjects — the paragraph needs both the buildings and the plants.
  • Paragraph 8 → A place that changed everything, twenty years before she settled there.
  • Paragraph 9 → Finding art in what the desert left behind.
  • Paragraph 12 → A wider world, and bigger canvases.

Watch out: paragraphs 9 and 10 both describe New Mexico subjects. The difference is what she painted — bones in one, the landscape itself in the other. Headings are usually separated by a single distinction like this.

Skill 5 — Sentence completion practice (extra)

Same passage, different task type. Complete each sentence with NO MORE THAN TWO WORDS AND/OR A NUMBER, then compare with the suggested answer.

  • Stieglitz’s gallery was on ______ in New York City. → Fifth Avenue
  • Over three decades Stieglitz gave her ______ solo exhibitions. → twenty-two
  • On her New Mexico trips she stayed for up to ______ at a time. → six months
  • Her Ghost Ranch retreat stood beneath cliffs ______ high. → 200 metres
  • The cloud canvas of 1965 was ______ in length. → 6 metres
  • She left a legacy of about ______. → 900 paintings

Skill 6 — Multiple choice practice (extra)

MCQ items on a biography almost always test a reason or a result, not a fact you can spot with your eyes. Choose, then check.

  • Why did enlarging tiny details make her flowers look abstract? → Because at that size the shapes and lines matter more than the object (‘Enlarging the tiniest details to fill an entire metre-wide canvas emphasized their shapes and lines and made them appear abstract’).
  • What was the immediate result of her 1929 visit to New Mexico? → It changed what she painted straight away (‘a lasting impact on her life, and an immediate effect on her work’).
  • Why did she return to New York every winter until 1949? → To exhibit her new work at Stieglitz’s gallery.
  • Why does the writer mention the artist’s age in 1965? → To show how demanding the six-metre canvas was — ‘a special feat for an artist nearing eighty years of age’.

Habit: for MCQ, find the sentence first and the option second. Options are written to sound like the passage; sentences are not.

Skill 7 — Yes / No / Not Given thinking (extra)

Q8–13 are TRUE/FALSE/NOT GIVEN because they test facts. These four test the writer’s claims instead — a useful contrast to feel the difference.

  • The writer admires O’Keeffe’s technical skill. → YES — ‘exceptionally keen powers of observation and great finesse with a paintbrush’.
  • The writer thinks Stieglitz was more important to her career than her own talent. → NOT GIVEN — his promotion is reported, never weighed against her ability.
  • The writer believes her late aerial paintings reflected a changed outlook. → YES — ‘reflecting perhaps her newly expanded view of the world’. The hedge perhaps makes it an opinion, not a fact.
  • The writer considers her influence on later artists to be over. → NO — her legacy ‘has continued to attract subsequent generations’.
