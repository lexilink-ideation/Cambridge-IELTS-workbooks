Reading Passage 2

Cambridge IELTS 20 — Test 3 — Reading Passage 2

Can the Planet’s Coral Reefs Be Saved? · Matching Headings + Multiple Choice + Sentence Completion (Q14–26)

📋 Passage Overview

This passage is a news-style report built around a new artificial coral reef at London Zoo. Sections A–C explain what the exhibit is, how corals actually work (tiny animals called polyps living with algae) and why the nickname ‘rainforests of the sea’ is disputed. Sections D–F turn to the threats — warming, bleaching, acidification, pollution — and then to the research at the zoo that is trying to find tougher corals and make them breed more often. The last section explains why showing this work to the public matters.

What the passage argues: That coral reefs are both extraordinarily valuable and extraordinarily fragile — they are the first part of the ocean to show that something is wrong — but that laboratory research into heat-tolerant species and artificial spawning, displayed to the public in exhibits like London Zoo’s Tiny Giants, gives a genuine reason for hope.

段落结构 Structure: A段：伦敦动物园新建人工珊瑚礁展馆 Tiny Giants，策展人提出两个目的（展示珊瑚之美 + 展示保护研究）。B段：珊瑚的生物学——水螅体（polyps）与藻类的共生关系，覆盖0.1%的海床却养活25%以上的海洋物种。C段：“海中雨林”这一比喻引发的争议，Attenborough 认为珊瑚礁更胜雨林。D段：威胁清单——热压力与白化、酸化、海平面上升、污染、缺氧、洋流变化；2050年前或损失90%以上。E段：动物园实验室的两项研究——寻找耐热耐酸物种、提高繁殖/产卵频率。F段：全球实验室网络与公众教育，结尾强调希望。

Task type: Q14–19 Matching Headings (six sections A–F, seven headings i–vii — one is never used), Q20–21 and Q22–23 Multiple Choice, choose TWO letters (both answers must be right to score both marks; order does not matter), and Q24–26 Sentence Completion — ONE WORD ONLY from the passage.

💡 Reading strategy tip: Three task types share one short passage, and they run in passage order, so never search from the top twice. For Matching Headings (Q14–19) read the first and last sentence of each section and ask ‘what is this section doing?’ — describing, disagreeing, warning, or hoping. For the two choose-TWO questions (Q20–23) tick options only when you can put your finger on the exact words; five plausible options are designed so that three of them are things you ‘know’ about reefs but the writer never says. For sentence completion (Q24–26) the limit is ONE WORD ONLY — copy the spelling from the passage, including the British spelling colour.
⚠️ Traps in this section 本节陷阱:
  • Heading i is a decoy — ‘Tried and tested solutions’ sounds perfect for Section E, but the research there is new and unfinished (‘scientists will be studying’, ‘we could consider’). Nothing in this passage has been tried and tested, so heading i is the one that stays unused.
  • Q20–21: what you know vs what is written — overfishing (B) and marine disease (D) are real reef problems and extreme storms (A) sound like climate change, but the writer’s list is exact: thermal stress, acidification, sea level increase, pollution by humans, deoxygenation and ocean current changes. Only C and E are in it.
  • Q22–23 option shift — in this question the distractor D (‘creating artificial reefs that corals could grow on’) borrows the words ‘artificial reef’ from Section A, where they describe a display tank, not a research project. Repeated words are the bait.
  • Q24 plural — the sentence says ‘a number of’, so the gap needs the plural tentacles. Copying the singular loses the mark even though the word is right.
  • Q25 wrong direction — the passage sentence gives two benefits at once: the algae get protection, the polyps get nutrients. Read carefully which partner the question is asking about. Q25 asks what the algae gain.
  • Q26 spelling — the passage is British English: colour, not ‘color’. Copy what is printed. Also note the verb: bleaching ‘strips reefs of their colour’ = ‘remove’.
  • Percentages that are not answers — 0.1%, 25% and 90% all appear within a few lines. Attach each number to its own idea: ocean bed covered / marine species housed / reefs that could be lost by 2050.

🔍 Complex Sentence Analysis

Academic Reading sentences are long because they stack clauses, not because the words are hard. Find the main verb first, then peel off the extra layers. 先找主句谓语动词，再逐层剥离修饰成分。
“These polyps are transparent but get their brilliant tones of pink, orange, blue, green, etc. from algae that live within them, which in turn get protection, while their photosynthesising of the sun’s rays provides nutrients for the polyps.”
Main ClauseThese polyps are transparent but get their brilliant tones… from algae.
Subordinate / Relative‘that live within them’ = a defining relative clause identifying the algae; ‘which in turn get protection’ = a non-defining clause about the algae; ‘while their photosynthesising… provides nutrients’ = a clause of simultaneous contrast.
Grammar NoteThe whole exchange is packed into one sentence: algae → colour and food for the polyps; polyps → protection for the algae. in turn signals the second half of a two-way deal, and is your marker for Q25.
Simple MeaningThe coral animals have no colour of their own. Algae inside them give them colour and food, and in return the algae are kept safe.
中文这些水螅体本身是透明的，其鲜艳的粉、橙、蓝、绿等色彩来自生活在其体内的藻类；藻类因此获得保护，而它们对阳光的光合作用又为水螅体提供养分。
“This comfortable symbiotic relationship has led to the growth of coral reefs that cover 0.1% of the planet’s ocean bed while providing homes for more than 25% of marine species.”
Main ClauseThis comfortable symbiotic relationship has led to the growth of coral reefs.
Subordinate / Relative‘that cover 0.1%…’ = a relative clause describing the reefs; ‘while providing homes…’ = a reduced clause of contrast (= although they provide homes).
Grammar Notelead to + noun is a cause-and-effect verb, not a verb of movement. The two percentages are deliberately unbalanced: a tiny area, a huge share of species — that contrast is the point.
Simple MeaningBecause coral and algae work together, reefs have grown. They cover almost none of the sea floor but shelter over a quarter of all sea life.
中文这种舒适的共生关系促成了珊瑚礁的生长；珊瑚礁仅覆盖全球海床的0.1%，却为超过25%的海洋物种提供了栖息地。
“As a result, coral reefs are often described as the ‘rainforests of the sea’, though the comparison is dismissed by some naturalists, including David Attenborough.”
Main ClauseCoral reefs are often described as the ‘rainforests of the sea’.
Subordinate / Relative‘though the comparison is dismissed…’ = a concession clause; ‘including David Attenborough’ = an exemplifying phrase.
Grammar NoteTwo passives in one sentence (‘are described’, ‘is dismissed’) because the people doing it are unimportant or general. though flips the sentence — and that flip is exactly what heading iv (‘Disagreement about the accuracy of a certain phrase’) describes.
Simple MeaningPeople often call reefs the rainforests of the sea, but some experts, such as David Attenborough, say the comparison is wrong.
中文因此，珊瑚礁常被称为“海中雨林”，不过包括大卫·阿滕伯勒在内的一些博物学家并不认同这一比喻。
“Unfortunately, these majestic sights are now under very serious threat, with the most immediate problem coming in the form of thermal stress.”
Main ClauseThese majestic sights are now under very serious threat.
Subordinate / Relative‘with the most immediate problem coming…’ = a with + noun + present participle structure adding detail.
Grammar NoteThe with + -ing pattern is a compact academic way of adding a second fact without a new sentence. most immediate means ‘first in time’, not ‘most serious’ — the rest of the paragraph then lists the others.
Simple MeaningSadly, reefs are in great danger, and the first danger to arrive is heat.
中文遗憾的是，这些壮观的景象如今正面临严重威胁，其中最迫在眉睫的问题是热压力。
“Scientists advise that more than 90% of reefs could be lost by 2050 unless urgent action is taken to tackle global heating and greenhouse gas emissions.”
Main ClauseScientists advise that more than 90% of reefs could be lost by 2050.
Subordinate / Relative‘unless urgent action is taken…’ = a negative conditional clause; ‘to tackle global heating…’ = an infinitive of purpose.
Grammar Noteunless = if… not. Note the hedging: could be lost, scientists advise. The writer reports a prediction, he does not state a fact — useful for Yes/No/Not Given thinking.
Simple MeaningExperts warn that if we do not act now on climate change, over nine reefs in ten may be gone by 2050.
中文科学家提醒，除非立即采取行动应对全球变暖和温室气体排放，否则到2050年可能有超过90%的珊瑚礁消失。
“However, aquarium-based research has enabled some corals to spawn artificially, which can assist coral reef restoration efforts.”
Main ClauseAquarium-based research has enabled some corals to spawn artificially.
Subordinate / Relative‘which can assist coral reef restoration efforts’ = a non-defining relative clause commenting on the whole idea.
Grammar Noteenable + object + to do is the standard academic ‘make it possible’ pattern (compare: allow, permit, help). This is the sentence behind the Q22–23 option about speeding up reproduction.
Simple MeaningResearch in tanks has made it possible for some corals to release eggs artificially, which helps rebuild damaged reefs.
中文然而，基于水族馆的研究已使某些珊瑚能够人工产卵，这有助于珊瑚礁的修复工作。

🧠 Reading Logic Training

IELTS Reading tests whether you follow the relationship between ideas: cause, contrast, example, definition, sequence and the writer’s own attitude. 抓住句子之间的逻辑关系，比逐词翻译更重要。

Cause & effect — the chain that kills a reef

Rising ocean temperatures → thermal stress → bleaching events → loss of colour → death. The mechanism is hidden in Section B: the polyps are transparent and the colour belongs to the algae, so when heat drives the algae out, the reef goes white and starves. Sections B and D therefore have to be read together — that link is what makes Q26 (colour) findable.

Contrast — ‘though’, ‘however’ and ‘unfortunately’ mark the turns

Each of these words turns the passage: ‘though the comparison is dismissed’ (Section C becomes about disagreement), ‘Unfortunately, these majestic sights are now under… threat’ (Section D becomes the warning), ‘However, scientists are trying to pinpoint…’ (Section E becomes the response). In a headings task, the sentence after the turn word usually contains the heading.

Comparison — the disputed metaphor

‘The rainforests of the sea’ is a comparison, and Attenborough attacks it by comparing the two experiences: in a rainforest the animals are hidden in the trees; on a reef the life is visible in front of you. Notice that the passage never says which ecosystem is objectively richer — it reports an opinion. A True/False item claiming ‘reefs contain more species than rainforests’ would be NOT GIVEN.

Examples — lists are where choose-TWO answers hide

Two lists matter. Threats: ‘ocean acidification, sea level increase, pollution by humans, deoxygenation and ocean current changes’ — that is where Q20–21 (C and E) live. Research aims: find species that survive warm acidic water, and increase breeding rates — that is where Q22–23 (B and D) live. Bracket lists as you skim; they are answer-dense.

Definition & apposition — free explanations inside commas

“tiny animals, known as polyps”; “the zoo’s new gallery, Tiny Giants, which is dedicated to…”; “Paul Pearce-Kelly, senior curator of invertebrates and fish”. You never need a dictionary for these words — the commas define them. This habit alone saves two or three minutes per passage.

Problem → solution — the shape of the whole text

Sections A–C set up the value of reefs, Section D states the problem, Sections E–F give the response: laboratory research, a global network of linked centres, and public display. Matching Headings is far easier once you have labelled each section value / problem / response in the margin.

Modality & hedging — what is certain and what is not

Compare ‘Coral reefs are dying’ (fact) with ‘more than 90% could be lost’, ‘we could consider the launching of coral-spawning programmes’ and ‘scientists will be studying’ (predictions and plans). Nothing in Section E has been proved yet — which is precisely why heading i, ‘Tried and tested solutions’, is a trap.

The writer’s attitude — alarmed but deliberately hopeful

The passage ends on the curator’s words: progress ‘can be shown to the public and encourage them to believe that we can do something’. The final section is not new science; it is about morale. That is why its heading is vi — Promoting hope, and why an option saying the reefs are now safe would be wrong for the same reason.

🎯 Question-Type Skill Practice

Skill 1 — Matching Headings (Q14–19)

Six sections, seven headings: one heading is never used. Ask what each section does, not what it mentions.

  • 14. Section A → v — Two clear educational goals. The curator states exactly two aims: ‘we want to show people how wonderful they are’ and ‘we also want to highlight the research and conservation efforts’. The word also gives you the ‘two’.
  • 15. Section B → ii — Cooperation beneath the waves. The whole section is the polyp–algae deal: ‘this comfortable symbiotic relationship’. Cooperation = symbiosis.
  • 16. Section C → iv — Disagreement about the accuracy of a certain phrase. The phrase is ‘the rainforests of the sea’; the disagreement is ‘the comparison is dismissed by some naturalists’.
  • 17. Section D → vii — A warning of further trouble ahead. ‘And that is just the start’, followed by a list of other menaces and the 2050 prediction.
  • 18. Section E → iii — Working to lessen the problems. Two research aims: find corals that survive warm acidic water, and raise breeding rates.
  • 19. Section F → vi — Promoting hope. ‘encourage them to believe that we can do something to save the planet’s reefs’.
  • Not used: i — Tried and tested solutions. Section E is future-tense research, not proven practice.

Habit to build: two headings here (iii and i) both sound like ‘solutions’, and two (v and vi) both sound like ‘the public’. Choose by evidence, and always leave the hardest section until the others are fixed.

Skill 2 — Multiple Choice, choose TWO (Q20–21): causes of damage

You must select two letters, and you only score if both are right. Work by elimination against the writer’s actual list in Section D.

  • A a rising number of extreme storms → not mentioned. ‘Wave erosion’ appears, but as a condition corals already survive — not as a growing threat.
  • B the removal of too many fish from the sea → not mentioned. Overfishing is a real-world reef problem, but this writer never raises it. Outside knowledge is the enemy here.
  • C the contamination of the sea from waste → CORRECT. Passage: ‘pollution by humans’. Contamination = pollution.
  • D increased disease among marine species → not mentioned. The passage says corals die, not that disease spreads.
  • E alterations in the usual flow of water in the seas → CORRECT. Passage: ‘ocean current changes’. Alterations = changes; flow of water = currents.

Technique: find the sentence that contains the list, then tick options off against it one at a time. Never choose an option because it is true in the world.

Skill 3 — Multiple Choice, choose TWO (Q22–23): the London Zoo researchers

This question sends you to one paragraph only — the second half of Section E, where the curator lists what the labs will do.

  • A expanding the number of different corals bred in laboratories → wrong. The aim is more frequent spawning, not a wider range of species in the labs. ‘All species’ is mentioned only as a hypothetical extension.
  • B identifying corals that cope well with changed sea conditions → CORRECT. ‘pinpoint hardy types of coral that could survive our overheated oceans’ and ‘find those that can survive best in warm, acidic waters’.
  • C creating artificial reefs for corals to grow on → wrong. The artificial reef in Section A is an exhibition tank, not a research technique. Word-match trap.
  • D methods that would speed up reproduction in some corals → CORRECT. ‘increase coral breeding rates’; corals spawn once a year, but artificial spawning could happen ‘several times a year’.
  • E materials that might protect reefs from higher temperatures → wrong. The plan is to find tougher corals, not protective materials.

Check before you move on: both correct options can be supported by a quotation. If you can only quote for one of your two letters, one of them is wrong.

Skill 4 — Sentence Completion (Q24–26): ONE WORD ONLY

These three sentences follow the passage in order, and all three answers sit in Sections B and D. Decide the word type before you look.

  • Q24 tentacles — ‘a number of…’ needs a plural countable noun. Passage: ‘polyps, with tentacles for capturing small marine creatures’. Capturing = collecting their food.
  • Q25 protection — ‘gain…’ needs an uncountable noun. Passage: the algae ‘in turn get protection’. Do not write ‘nutrients’ — those go to the polyps, not the algae.
  • Q26 colour — ‘remove the… from coral’ needs a noun. Passage: bleaching events ‘strip reefs of their colour’. British spelling; one word only, so not ‘their colour’.

Tip: in sentence completion the grammar of the printed sentence is a free clue. Read your finished sentence aloud — if it sounds wrong, the word is wrong.

Skill 5 — True / False / Not Given thinking (extra practice)

This passage does not include a T/F/NG task, but it is perfect for training the judgement. Decide first, then read the reason.

  • The Tiny Giants gallery focuses on large sea creatures. → FALSE — it is dedicated to minuscule invertebrate creatures; the name is deliberately ironic.
  • Coral polyps would have no colour without algae. → TRUE — ‘These polyps are transparent but get their brilliant tones… from algae’.
  • David Attenborough believes reefs are more beautiful than rainforests. → TRUE — ‘if you want beauty and wildlife, you want a coral reef’.
  • Rainforests contain fewer species than coral reefs. → NOT GIVEN — the passage compares the experience of visiting them, never the species counts.
  • Corals in the wild reproduce more than once a year. → FALSE — ‘Coral spawn just once a year’; several times a year is only a future possibility in aquariums.
  • London Zoo’s laboratory results are shared with other research centres. → TRUE — ‘the London Zoo centre linked to this global network. Studies carried out in one centre can then be tested in others.’

Skill 6 — Locating information fast (extra)

Which section would you scan for each idea? In a six-section passage, getting to the right block in ten seconds is worth more than reading quickly.

  • The size of the tank window → Section A (‘seven-metre-wide window’).
  • What corals eat and how they catch it → Section B (tentacles, small marine creatures).
  • The proportion of sea life that depends on reefs → Section B (more than 25%).
  • An expert rejecting a popular nickname → Section C.
  • A prediction with a date → Section D (90% by 2050).
  • Why corals are described as an early-warning system → end of Section D (‘corals will be the first to react’).

Corals are colonies of tiny animals called polyps, which use their tentacles to catch food. Because the polyps are transparent, the colour of a reef comes from the algae inside them; in exchange for protection, the algae supply nutrients through photosynthesis. This symbiotic partnership built reefs that shelter over a quarter of marine species. Today warmer seas cause bleaching, which strips reefs of their colour, and further menaces include acidification, pollution, deoxygenation and changes to ocean currents.
