Reading Passage 1

Cambridge IELTS 20 — Test 2 — Reading Passage 1

Manatees · Note Completion + True / False / Not Given (Q1–13)

📋 Passage Overview

This passage is a factual description of the manatee, or sea cow — a fully aquatic mammal in the group Sirenia. The first half is pure biology: how manatees differ from dugongs, why their short neck forces them to turn their whole body, what they eat, how their teeth and lips work, and how they breathe and control their buoyancy. The second half becomes a species-by-species survey (West Indian, African, Amazonian) and finishes with conservation status: hunting, fishing nets, plastic and boat strikes. Because the structure is so predictable, the hardest part is not understanding it — it is staying in the right paragraph.

What the passage argues: That the manatee’s whole body is shaped by a fully aquatic, plant-eating life — short neck, flippers instead of legs, endlessly replaced molars, hand-like lips, diaphragm-controlled buoyancy — and that all three species are now endangered, mainly because of human activity: hunting, fishing nets, plastic and boat strikes.

段落结构 Structure: 第1段：海牛属于 Sirenia，与儒艮的异同（尾巴形状是关键）+ 三个物种名称。第2段：颈椎只有六块、需转身才能看两侧、鳍状肢、体表细毛感知震动。第3段：食性——海草为主，每天进食约八小时，非洲海牛为杂食。第4段：牙齿全是臼齿、不断替换；用嘴唇代替门齿抓食。第5段：呼吸与浮力——清醒时2–4分钟换气、睡眠时15–20分钟；靠横膈膜储气调节浮力。第6–8段：三个物种的体型与分布（西印度海牛及其两个亚种、非洲海牛、亚马逊海牛）。第9–10段：保护现状——IUCN 等级、历史捕杀、渔网与塑料、佛罗里达的船只撞击与限速法规。

Task type: Q1–6 Note completion — ONE WORD AND/OR A NUMBER from the passage, so every answer must be copied, not invented. Q7–13 True / False / Not Given — statements follow the order of the second half of the passage.

💡 Reading strategy tip: Two tasks, two completely different reading speeds. For the note completion (Q1–6) the headings Appearance / Movement / Feeding / Breathing follow the passage in order, so read the notes first, decide the word type for each gap (a body part? a plant? a physical property?), then scan for the heading topic rather than for the gap words. For True / False / Not Given (Q7–13) slow right down and test one word at a time. Four of these seven items turn on a single risky word: ‘variety’ (Q7), ‘warmer…than’ (Q8), ‘limited to coastal’ (Q9), ‘only revealed many years later’ (Q10), ‘increased significantly’ (Q12). If the passage never makes that exact claim — especially a comparison or a change over time — the answer is NOT GIVEN, no matter how reasonable the statement sounds.
⚠️ Traps in this section 本节陷阱:
  • Q1 is about shape, not size — dugongs and manatees are alike in ‘size, colour and shape’ and both have flippers. The one difference given is the tail: broad and rounded versus fluked. Only one noun in that sentence answers ‘differently shaped’.
  • Q3 hairs vs skin — the note already says ‘on their skin’, so ‘skin’ cannot be the answer. The passage prints the plural: ‘some hairs covering their bodies’.
  • Q5 lips, not teeth — the same paragraph is full of teeth (molars, incisors). But the note says ‘grasp and pull up plants’, and the passage says manatees have lips ‘which function like a pair of hands’ instead of incisors.
  • Q8 is a comparison that is never made — the passage says the Florida manatee takes refuge in warm water below 20°C, but it never compares Florida and Antillean water temperatures. A comparison the writer does not make is NOT GIVEN.
  • Q10 and Q12 are about knowledge and trends, not facts — the passage gives a figure for the 1935–54 killings (Q10) and a count of cases in 2009–2020 (Q12), but it says nothing about when this was discovered or whether the risk rose. Reporting a number is not reporting a change.
  • Numbers that are not answers — six neck bones, seven, 15%, eight hours, 2–4 minutes, 10–12 hours, 15–20 minutes, 3.5 m, 500 kg, 2.5 m, 350 kg, 20°C, 23°C, 140,000, 1,800, 700, 20%, 40 years. Attach each figure to its noun before you use it.

🔍 Complex Sentence Analysis

Academic Reading sentences are long because they stack clauses, not because the words are hard. Find the main verb first, then peel off the extra layers. 先找主句谓语动词，再逐层剥离修饰成分。
“However, the manatee has a broad, rounded tail, whereas the dugong’s is fluked, like that of a whale.”
Main ClauseThe manatee has a broad, rounded tail.
Subordinate / Relative‘whereas the dugong’s is fluked’ = a clause of contrast; ‘like that of a whale’ = a comparison phrase where that stands for the tail.
Grammar Notewhereas always signals a difference between two things already mentioned. Note the two substitutions: the dugong’s = the dugong’s tail, and that of a whale = the tail of a whale. English avoids repeating the noun.
Simple MeaningThe manatee’s tail is wide and round; the dugong’s tail is split like a whale’s.
中文然而，海牛的尾巴宽而圆，而儒艮的尾巴则像鲸鱼那样呈分叉状。
“This short neck allows a manatee to move its head up and down, but not side to side.”
Main ClauseThis short neck allows a manatee to move its head.
Subordinate / Relative‘up and down, but not side to side’ = two adverbial phrases joined by a contrast.
Grammar NotePattern: allow + object + to + verb. Notice how much information sits in the tiny word but: it converts a description of ability into a description of limitation, which is what the next sentence then explains.
Simple MeaningBecause its neck is short, a manatee can nod but cannot look left or right.
中文这种短颈使海牛的头部可以上下移动，却无法左右转动。
“Due to manatees’ abrasive aquatic plant diet, these teeth get worn down and they eventually fall out, so they continually grow new teeth that get pushed forward to replace the ones they lose.”
Main ClauseThese teeth get worn down and they eventually fall out. / They continually grow new teeth.
Subordinate / Relative‘Due to… diet’ = cause; ‘so…’ = result; ‘that get pushed forward…’ = a defining relative clause describing the new teeth.
Grammar NoteOne sentence, three logical steps: cause (due to) → effect (worn down, fall out) → consequence (so… grow new teeth). ‘The ones they lose’ uses ones to avoid repeating ‘teeth’.
Simple MeaningRough plants wear the teeth away until they drop out, so new teeth keep growing and moving forward to take their place.
中文由于海牛以粗糙的水生植物为食，这些牙齿会被磨损并最终脱落，因此它们会不断长出新牙并向前推移，以替换掉落的牙齿。
“It moves between fresh water and salt water, taking advantage of coastal mangroves and coral reefs, rivers, lakes and inland lagoons.”
Main ClauseIt moves between fresh water and salt water.
Subordinate / Relative‘taking advantage of…’ = a present-participle phrase adding how/what it does at the same time.
Grammar NoteA reduced clause: the full form would be ‘and it takes advantage of…’. The list that follows is deliberately long — five habitat types — and that list is exactly what makes Question 7 TRUE.
Simple MeaningThe West Indian manatee lives in both rivers and the sea, using mangroves, reefs, rivers, lakes and lagoons.
中文它在淡水与海水之间往返，利用沿海的红树林与珊瑚礁、河流、湖泊以及内陆潟湖。
“The species also makes use of rivers, with the mammals seen in landlocked countries such as Mali and Niger.”
Main ClauseThe species also makes use of rivers.
Subordinate / Relative‘with the mammals seen in landlocked countries…’ = a with + noun + past participle structure giving supporting evidence.
Grammar NoteThe with + object + participle pattern is a compact way of attaching proof to a claim. Here it is the proof that kills Question 9: a landlocked country has no coast, so the African manatee’s range is not ‘limited to coastal waters’.
Simple MeaningAfrican manatees also live in rivers, and have been seen in inland countries like Mali and Niger.
中文该物种也利用河流，人们曾在马里和尼日尔等内陆国家见到这种哺乳动物。
“However, laws in certain parts of Florida now limit boat speeds during winter, allowing slow-moving manatees more time to respond.”
Main ClauseLaws in certain parts of Florida now limit boat speeds during winter.
Subordinate / Relative‘allowing slow-moving manatees more time to respond’ = a participle clause of result.
Grammar NoteThree hedging details make Question 13 TRUE rather than overstated: certain parts (not all of Florida), during winter (not all year), and allowing more time (not preventing strikes). The question is worded just as carefully: ‘some legislation… aims to reduce the likelihood’.
Simple MeaningIn some parts of Florida, boats must go slowly in winter so that manatees have longer to get out of the way.
中文不过，佛罗里达部分地区的法律现已限制冬季船速，使行动缓慢的海牛有更多时间作出反应。

🧠 Reading Logic Training

IELTS Reading tests whether you follow the relationship between ideas: cause, contrast, example, definition, sequence and the writer’s own attitude. 抓住句子之间的逻辑关系，比逐词翻译更重要。

Contrast — ‘whereas’, ‘however’ and ‘unlike’ carry the answers

This passage builds almost every fact by comparison. “Dugongs and manatees look quite alike… However, the manatee has a broad, rounded tail, whereas the dugong’s is fluked” gives you Q1; “Unlike most mammals, manatees have only six bones in their neck” opens the movement paragraph. Train yourself to stop at these three words: the sentence after them is where the specific, testable fact lives.

Cause & effect — a chain that runs through one sentence

“Due to manatees’ abrasive aquatic plant diet, these teeth get worn down and they eventually fall out, so they continually grow new teeth.” Rough food → worn teeth → teeth lost → constant replacement. IELTS loves to test the middle of such a chain, so mark both the cause marker (due to) and the result marker (so) as you read.

Comparison — the exact trap in Question 8

The passage tells you the Florida manatee shelters in warm water when the ambient temperature drops below 20°C, and separately that the Antillean manatee ranges from the Bahamas to Brazil. It never puts the two water temperatures side by side. A statement that ranks two things — warmer than, larger than, more common than — is NOT GIVEN unless the writer makes that ranking. Compare this with the Amazonian manatee, where a real comparison is made: “the smallest species”.

Examples — ‘such as’ gives you a sample, and sometimes the answer

“…seen in landlocked countries such as Mali and Niger” is not decoration: it is the evidence that makes Q9 FALSE. Similarly “such as at the warm-water outfalls from powerplants” shows what ‘artificially warmed water’ means. Examples explain the general statement immediately before them — read them as definitions.

Definition & apposition — free explanations inside commas and dashes

“Manatees, also known as sea cows, are aquatic mammals…”; “Manatees’ teeth are all molars — flat, rounded teeth for grinding food”; “the dugong’s is fluked, like that of a whale”. You do not need to know molar or fluked — the writer defines them for you. Never look up a word before checking whether the punctuation has already explained it.

Problem → solution — the shape of the final paragraph

Problem: “The chief cause of death in Florida manatees is boat strikes.” Solution: “However, laws in certain parts of Florida now limit boat speeds during winter, allowing slow-moving manatees more time to respond.” That However flips the paragraph from bad news to a response, and the response is Q13. Whenever a problem is stated, expect a measure to follow, and expect a question about it.

Numbers and quantity — attach every figure to its noun

Six neck bones (manatee) vs seven (humans, giraffes); 15% of body weight eaten daily; eight hours grazing; 2–4 minutes between breaths when awake vs 15–20 minutes when asleep; 3.5 m / 500 kg (West Indian and African) vs 2.5 m / 350 kg (Amazonian); below 20°C vs above 23°C; 140,000 killed 1935–54; 1,800 cases reviewed, at least 700 involving manatees; a 20% decline over 40 years. Write the noun next to the number in the margin — that habit alone protects Q11 and Q12.

The writer’s stance — neutral reporting with careful hedging

The tone is scientific and cautious. Look at the hedges: “It’s thought that manatees use their muscular diaphragm”, “They may use diaphragm contractions”, “It is estimated that 140,000…”, “are expected to undergo a decline”. The writer never claims certainty about mechanisms or the future. In a Yes/No/Not Given task, an option stating these as proven facts would be wrong for exactly that reason.

🎯 Question-Type Skill Practice

Skill 1 — Note Completion (Q1–6): use the headings as a map

ONE WORD AND/OR A NUMBER from the passage. Predict the word type first, then scan for the heading topic, not for the gap.

  • Q1 tail (Appearance) — the note says ‘a differently shaped ___’. Everything else in that sentence is a similarity; the only difference given is broad and rounded versus fluked.
  • Q2 flippers (Movement) — ‘a manatee must turn its entire body, steering with its flippers’. The note asks what helps them turn around. Keep the plural.
  • Q3 hairs (Movement) — ‘some hairs covering their bodies which help them sense vibrations’. ‘Skin’ is already printed in the note, so it is ruled out.
  • Q4 seagrasses (Feeding) — ‘Seagrasses and other marine plants make up most of a manatee’s diet.’ The note’s ‘such as’ needs an example, and this is the only one named.
  • Q5 lips (Feeding) — ‘Instead of having incisors to grasp their food, manatees have lips which function like a pair of hands.’ The note’s ‘grasp and pull up’ matches ‘grasp… tear food away’.
  • Q6 buoyancy (Breathing) — ‘manatees use their muscular diaphragm and breathing to adjust their buoyancy’. ‘Regulate’ = ‘adjust’; the gap needs the property, not the muscle.

Check before you move on: read the completed note aloud. If it does not sound like grammatical English, your word is in the wrong form.

Skill 2 — TRUE / FALSE / NOT GIVEN (Q7–13): three questions, in order

Ask them in this sequence: (1) Does the passage talk about this? (2) Does it say the same thing? (3) Does it say the opposite? No to (1) = NOT GIVEN, yes to (3) = FALSE.

  • 7. West Indian manatees live in a variety of habitats. → TRUE — ‘mangroves and coral reefs, rivers, lakes and inland lagoons’ plus fresh and salt water. A list of five habitat types is exactly what ‘a variety’ means.
  • 8. The Florida manatee lives in warmer waters than the Antillean. → NOT GIVEN — temperature is given only for the Florida subspecies, and the two are never compared.
  • 9. The African manatee’s range is limited to coastal waters. → FALSE — ‘The species also makes use of rivers… seen in landlocked countries’. ‘Also’ contradicts ‘limited to’.
  • 10. The scale of the losses was only revealed years later. → NOT GIVEN — the figure of 140,000 is given, but nothing at all is said about when or how it became known.
  • 11. Populations are predicted to fall. → TRUE — ‘Both are also expected to undergo a decline of 20% over the next 40 years.’ ‘Expected to’ = ‘it is predicted’; ‘next 40 years’ = ‘the coming decades’.
  • 12. The risk increased significantly in 2009–2020. → NOT GIVEN — the review counted cases in that window; it never compares them with an earlier period.
  • 13. Some legislation aims to reduce boat strikes. → TRUE — ‘laws in certain parts of Florida now limit boat speeds during winter’. ‘Some’ matches ‘certain parts’ perfectly.

Habit to build: underline the single riskiest word in each statement — variety, warmer than, limited to, only revealed, predicted, increased significantly, some. That word, not the topic, decides the answer.

Skill 3 — Locating information fast

Which paragraph would you scan for each idea? Build the index once, then jump — never re-read from the top.

  • How manatees differ from dugongs → paragraph 1.
  • Why they cannot look sideways → paragraph 2 (six neck bones).
  • How they detect movement in the water → end of paragraph 2 (hairs).
  • How much they eat each day → paragraph 3 (15% of body weight, eight hours).
  • Why their teeth keep being replaced → paragraph 4 (abrasive diet).
  • How long they can stay under water → paragraph 5 (2–4 and 15–20 minutes).
  • Where each species lives → paragraphs 6–8, one species each.
  • IUCN categories and historical hunting → paragraph 9.
  • Fishing nets, plastic and boat strikes → the final paragraph.

Tip: in a species-by-species passage, write the species name beside each of the last few paragraphs during your skim. Q8 and Q9 then take seconds.

Skill 5 — Matching headings practice (extra)

  • Paragraph 5 → Breathing and staying afloat — not ‘Sleeping habits’, a detail inside it.
  • Paragraph 6 → The most widely distributed species.
  • Final paragraph → Human threats and one legal response.

Watch out: paragraphs 9 and 10 both discuss conservation status. The difference is which species and which threat — historical hunting versus modern nets, plastic and boats.

Skill 6 — Yes / No / Not Given thinking (extra)

These statements are about the writer’s claims rather than plain facts. Decide before you read the reason.

  • Scientists are certain how manatees control their buoyancy. → NO — the writer hedges twice: ‘It’s thought that…’ and ‘They may use…’.
  • Hunting is still legal somewhere in the African manatee’s range. → NO — ‘Manatee hunting is now illegal in every country the African species is found in.’
  • Boat speed limits have already reduced manatee deaths. → NOT GIVEN — the laws exist and their purpose is stated, but no result is reported.
  • Amazonian manatees are the smallest of the three species. → YES — stated directly, and supported by 2.5 m / 350 kg against 3.5 m / 500 kg.

Review Quiz

Q1
B. The shape of the tail.
They are alike in size, colour and shape and both have flippers. ‘However, the manatee has a broad, rounded tail, whereas the dugong’s is fluked’ — Question 1.
Q2
B. Its neck has six bones instead of seven, so it cannot turn side to side.
‘This short neck allows a manatee to move its head up and down, but not side to side.’ The flippers then do the steering — Question 2.
Q3
C. Help it sense vibrations in the water.
‘They also have some hairs covering their bodies which help them sense vibrations in the water around them’ — Question 3. Note that ‘skin’ is already printed in the note.
Q4
B. African manatees also eat molluscs and fish.
‘African manatees are omnivorous — studies have shown that molluscs and fish make up a small part of their diets.’ The West Indian and Amazonian species are the herbivores.
Q5
B. Because the note asks what grasps and pulls up plants, and the lips do that instead of incisors.
‘Instead of having incisors to grasp their food, manatees have lips which function like a pair of hands to help tear food away from the seafloor.’
Q6
C. Every 15–20 minutes.
Adults sleep underwater for 10–12 hours a day but surface every 15–20 minutes. The 2–4 minute figure applies when they are awake.
Q7
B. Because it uses fresh and salt water, mangroves, reefs, rivers, lakes and lagoons.
The list of five habitat types plus the movement between fresh and salt water is exactly what ‘a variety of different aquatic habitats’ means. Option C describes the African manatee.
Q8
C. Because a temperature is given only for the Florida subspecies and no comparison is made.
A comparison the writer never makes is always NOT GIVEN. Do not build the ranking yourself from geography.
Q9
B. They have been seen in landlocked countries such as Mali and Niger.
A landlocked country has no coast, so the range is not ‘limited to coastal waters’. ‘The species also makes use of rivers’ is the contradiction.
Q10
D. That the risk to manatees rose during that period.
This is Question 12. Counting cases in a period is not the same as showing an increase across it — there is no earlier figure to compare with, so the answer is NOT GIVEN.
Q11
B. Boat strikes; some areas now limit boat speeds in winter.
Question 13. ‘The chief cause of death in Florida manatees is boat strikes. However, laws in certain parts of Florida now limit boat speeds during winter.’
Q12
C. Neutral and factual, using hedges such as ‘it’s thought that’ and ‘may’.
The buoyancy mechanism is introduced with ‘It’s thought that’ and ‘They may use’, and the future decline with ‘are expected to’. The writer reports rather than argues.
