Reading Passage 3

Cambridge IELTS 20 — Test 4 — Reading Passage 3

A New Role for Livestock Guard Dogs · Matching Information + Matching People + Summary Completion (Q27–40)

📋 Passage Overview

This passage tells the story of a job that came back. For thousands of years big dogs guarded sheep and goats from wolves and bears; when those predators were wiped out in the 19th and 20th centuries the dogs became unemployed — and now that conservation has brought the predators back, the dogs are needed again. The twist in the title is that the dogs now protect the predators too: a farmer who is not losing animals has no reason to shoot a wolf or a cheetah. The second half of the passage is carefully balanced, listing the doubts (self-reported figures, predators simply pushed next door) and the side effects (more jackals killed, diseases spread, competition for food) before ending on a hopeful note.

What the passage argues: That livestock guard dogs are now a conservation tool as well as a farming tool — fewer dead sheep means fewer dead wolves and cheetahs. The writer accepts the evidence is strong but insists on two qualifications: much of it is self-reported, and protecting one predator can quietly harm other species.

段落结构 Structure: 第A段：历史背景——捕食者被消灭后护畜犬失业；随着野生动物保护，捕食者回归，护畜犬"复出"。第B段：全球约50个犬种；从小与畜群一起长大，挡在畜群与威胁之间、吠叫、驱赶；训练要点（Macon）。第C段：效果证据——葡萄牙Ribeiro：90%以上农户评价"很好或极佳"；澳大利亚van Bommel与Johnson：65%以上牧民称捕食完全停止。第D段：新角色——保护捕食者本身；纳米比亚猎豹案例；Young提出"共同利益"。第E段：质疑——Smith指出88%的数据属自我报告，须谨慎；Ribeiro指出压力可能转移给邻居。第F段：非预期的生态影响——豺被杀数量上升、传播疾病、与其他食肉动物争食、制造"恐惧景观"。第G段：正面外溢效应——保护地面筑巢鸟类；澳大利亚用犬提升生物多样性；对未来审慎乐观。

Task type: Q27–31 Matching information to paragraphs A–G — NB: you may use any letter more than once; Q32–36 Matching statements to five named people A–E, each used once; Q37–40 Summary completion — ONE WORD ONLY from the passage, drawn from paragraphs F and G in order.

💡 Reading strategy tip: This is a classic Passage 3 shape: A–C evidence for, D the new idea, E–F objections, G the balanced ending. Label the seven paragraphs with those five words before you start and Q27–31 become almost mechanical. Two warnings. First, the NB above Q27–31 tells you a letter may be used more than once — here paragraph B answers two questions, so never cross a letter off. Second, Q32–36 are matching-people questions: every answer is inside a pair of quotation marks, so find the five quotes first and match statements to quotes, not to paragraphs. The summary at the end (Q37–40) draws only on paragraphs F and G and follows their order exactly.
⚠️ Traps in this section 本节陷阱:
  • Q29 and Q31 are both paragraph B. The NB line exists precisely because of this. Q29 wants the dogs’ methods (standing between livestock and threat, barking, chasing, mere presence); Q31 wants how they gain their skills (raised from an early age with the animals; Macon on initial training). Two different sentences, one paragraph.
  • Q30 says “academics”, plural. Paragraph D also contains an academic (Julie Young at Utah State University) with a positive claim, but only one. Paragraph C has Silvia Ribeiro in Portugal and van Bommel and Johnson in Australia, all reporting protection of herds. Count the researchers before you choose.
  • Q27 “one predator… protected” is not paragraph G. In G the dogs protect ground-nesting birds from foxes — birds are prey, and the fox is the animal being kept away. The predator that gets protected is the Namibian cheetah in paragraph D, because farmers stopped killing them.
  • Q37 is jackals, not cheetahs. The summary sentence says deaths of other predators rose. Paragraph F: ‘in spite of the impact… on cheetahs, the number of jackals killed by dogs and people actually increased.’ The word ‘cheetahs’ sits in the same sentence as a distractor.
  • Q40 needs an animal, not a bird. ‘predators such as ______’ — the passage says the nests are in fields ‘where foxes would normally raid them’. Students who are still thinking about Namibia sometimes write ‘jackals’ again.
  • Q32 vs Q34 — two people talking about killing predators. Young (D) is positive: no dead cow means no permit to kill a wolf, so both livestock and wildlife are saved. Ribeiro (E) is negative: if only some farmers have dogs, the pressure moves to the neighbour, who may poison a whole pack. Same topic, opposite direction.

ranchern.a farmer with a large area of land for cattle or sheep（美）大牧场主“If ranchers don’t have a dead cow, they will not make a call to apply for a permit…”American English; the British/Australian equivalents in this passage are farmer and herder.

self-reportedadj.given by the people themselves rather than measured independently自我报告的“…warned that such self-reported results must be taken with a pinch of salt.”Key to Q33. The question paraphrase is ‘claims… may not be totally accurate’.

unintendedadj.not planned or wanted非预期的；意外的“Another concern is whether there may be unintended ecological effects…”The heading idea for the Q37–40 summary: ‘Unintended ecological effects of using guard dogs’.

🔍 Complex Sentence Analysis

Academic Reading sentences are long because they stack clauses, not because the words are hard. Find the main verb first, then peel off the extra layers. 先找主句谓语动词，再逐层剥离修饰成分。
“But in the 19th and 20th centuries, when such predators were largely exterminated, most guard dogs lost their jobs.”
Main ClauseMost guard dogs lost their jobs.
Subordinate / Relative‘in the 19th and 20th centuries’ = a time phrase; ‘when such predators were largely exterminated’ = a non-defining relative clause with when, explaining the cause.
Grammar NoteThe relative clause is doing the work of a because clause: the dogs lost their jobs because the predators were gone. Note the passive ‘were exterminated’ — the writer does not name who did it, though it was humans.
Simple MeaningPeople killed off nearly all the wolves and bears in the 1800s and 1900s, so most guard dogs were no longer needed.
中文但在19世纪和20世纪，这类捕食者基本被消灭殆尽，大多数护畜犬也因此失去了工作。
“If necessary, they will chase away predators, but often their mere presence is sufficient.”
Main ClauseThey will chase away predators. / Their mere presence is sufficient.
Subordinate / Relative‘If necessary’ = a reduced conditional clause (= if it is necessary), placed first for emphasis.
Grammar NoteTwo options are contrasted by but: active defence versus passive deterrence. Key to Q29: the question asks about ‘the methods used… to keep predators away’, and this sentence plus the previous one (standing between the livestock and any threat, barking loudly) is the full answer.
Simple MeaningThe dogs will drive predators off when they have to, but usually just being there is enough.
中文必要时它们会驱赶捕食者，但通常仅仅是它们在场就已足够。
“A fluffy white puppy is fun to be around, but too much human affection makes it a great dog for guarding the front porch, rather than a great livestock guard dog.”
Main ClauseToo much human affection makes it a great dog for guarding the front porch.
Subordinate / Relative‘A fluffy white puppy is fun to be around’ = a concession before the but; ‘rather than a great livestock guard dog’ = a contrasting phrase closing the sentence.
Grammar Notemake + object + noun phrase (makes it a great dog) is the causative pattern. Key to Q36: guarding the front porch = a house guard dog, so the two kinds of training need a different focus.
Simple MeaningA cuddly puppy is nice, but if people give it too much love it becomes a house dog, not a flock dog.
中文毛茸茸的白色小狗很讨人喜欢，但过多的人类亲昵会让它成为看门的好狗，而不是优秀的护畜犬。
“It is hoped that reductions in livestock losses can make farmers more tolerant of predators and less likely to kill them.”
Main ClauseIt is hoped that… (impersonal main clause)
Subordinate / Relative‘that reductions in livestock losses can make farmers more tolerant… and less likely…’ = a noun clause functioning as the real subject.
Grammar NoteIt is hoped that… is an impersonal passive: nobody is named as hoping, so the claim stays cautious. Notice the two parallel results after make farmers: more tolerant of, less likely to. Different prepositions, same structure.
Simple MeaningPeople hope that if farmers lose fewer animals, they will accept predators more and kill them less.
中文人们希望，牲畜损失的减少能让农民对捕食者更加宽容，也更不容易去猎杀它们。
“Looking at all the published evidence, Bethany Smith at Nottingham Trent University in the UK found that up to 88 per cent of farmers said they no longer killed predators after using dogs — but warned that such self-reported results must be taken with a pinch of salt.”
Main ClauseBethany Smith found that up to 88 per cent of farmers said they no longer killed predators — but warned that…
Subordinate / Relative‘Looking at all the published evidence’ = a present-participle clause of manner; two that-clauses hang off the parallel verbs found and warned.
Grammar NoteThe sentence structure itself is the answer to Q33: the writer gives you a big number and then immediately undercuts it with but warned. Watch the hedge up to 88 per cent — that is a maximum, not an average.
Simple MeaningAfter reviewing all the studies, Smith found that as many as 88% of farmers claimed to have stopped killing predators, but she said we should not fully trust figures that people report about themselves.
中文在查阅了所有已发表的证据后，英国诺丁汉特伦特大学的Bethany Smith发现，高达88%的农民表示使用护畜犬后不再猎杀捕食者，但她提醒说，这类自我报告的结果不可全信。
“For example, their presence has been found to protect birds which build their nests on the ground in fields, where foxes would normally raid them.”
Main ClauseTheir presence has been found to protect birds.
Subordinate / Relative‘which build their nests on the ground in fields’ = a defining relative clause identifying which birds; ‘where foxes would normally raid them’ = a relative clause of place attached to fields.
Grammar NoteTwo relative clauses chain one after the other — a very common Passage 3 pattern. Key to Q40: the summary says ‘threatened by predators such as ______’, and the animal inside the second relative clause is foxes. The conditional would normally means ‘in the usual situation, without dogs’.
Simple MeaningFor instance, having dogs around protects birds that nest on the ground in fields, where foxes would usually steal from the nests.
中文例如，人们发现犬只的存在能保护那些在田间地面筑巢的鸟类——在没有犬只的情况下，狐狸通常会袭击这些鸟巢。

🧠 Reading Logic Training

IELTS Reading tests whether you follow the relationship between ideas: cause, contrast, example, definition, sequence and the writer’s own attitude. 抓住句子之间的逻辑关系，比逐词翻译更重要。

Cause & effect — a chain that runs the whole length of the passage

Follow it in one line: predators exterminated → dogs unemployed → conservation brings predators back → dogs re-employed → livestock losses fall → farmers stop shooting predators → predators are protected. The hinge sentence is Julie Young’s: “If ranchers don’t have a dead cow, they will not make a call to apply for a permit to kill a wolf.” That one if-sentence is the argument of the entire article, and it is also the answer to Q32.

Contrast — ‘but’ is where the marks are

Count the reversals: “But in the 19th and 20th centuries…”, “In recent years, however…”, “but often their mere presence is sufficient”, “But today’s guard dogs also have a new role”, “but warned that such self-reported results…”, “The evidence so far, however, indicates that these consequences aren’t always negative.” Five of the seven paragraphs turn on a contrast word, and four of them sit next to an answer.

Comparison — percentages that look alike but measure different things

Four figures use ‘90 per cent’ or similar and they measure four different things: 90%+ of Portuguese farmers rated the dogs highly; 65%+ of Australian herders saw predation stop; 90%+ of Namibian cheetahs live outside protected areas; 90%+ of Namibian farmers reported fewer losses; up to 88% of farmers said they no longer killed predators. Never match a question to a number — match it to what the number counts.

Examples — ‘For example’ and ‘Indeed’ mark the evidence layer

Paragraph C opens ‘For example, in Portugal…’ and paragraph G says ‘For example, their presence has been found to protect birds… Indeed, Australian researchers are now using dogs to enhance biodiversity’. Indeed does not mean ‘in fact’ in the Chinese sense of correcting something — it strengthens the point just made. Q28 (an optimistic suggestion) lives in exactly this strengthening move.

Definition & apposition — who each expert is, given free

Every name arrives with its credentials attached: ‘Dan Macon, a shepherd with three guard dogs’; ‘biologist Silvia Ribeiro’; ‘Linda van Bommel and Chris Johnson at the University of Tasmania’; ‘Julie Young, at Utah State University in the US’; ‘Bethany Smith at Nottingham Trent University in the UK’. Note who is not an academic: Macon is a working shepherd. Q30 asks for ‘different academics’, which quietly excludes his paragraph.

Problem → solution → new problem

The passage does not stop at the solution. Paragraph D gives the good news; paragraph E raises doubt about the evidence (self-reported) and about displacement (the neighbour poisons a whole pack); paragraph F raises doubt about other species (jackals, diseases, competition for food, a landscape of fear). This three-step shape is standard in academic writing, and in IELTS it is usually where the Yes/No/Not Given or multiple-choice questions are set.

Chronology — four time markers organise the argument

‘For thousands of years’ (the old job) → ‘in the 19th and 20th centuries’ (the job disappears) → ‘In recent years’ / ‘Today’ (the job returns) → ‘In 2015’ (the jackal study) → ‘The evidence so far’ and ‘are now using’ (the present, still unfinished). Q26-style questions about ‘in recent years’ always attach to the third of these markers.

The writer’s attitude — supportive, but scrupulously balanced

The writer clearly likes the idea, yet every positive claim is hedged: ‘It is hoped that…’, ‘Young believes this result applies widely’, ‘it is possible that…’, ‘The extent of this problem isn’t known’, ‘so if we can get this right, there may be a bright future’. That final conditional is the whole attitude in nine words: optimistic, but conditional on doing it properly.

🎯 Question-Type Skill Practice

Skill 1 — Matching Information to Paragraphs (Q27–31)

Read the NB line first: a letter may be used more than once. Turn each question into one keyword, then check your seven-paragraph map.

  • 27. An example of one predator being protected by guard dogs → D. Keyword: protected predator. In Namibia more than 90% of farmers reported a dramatic reduction in losses ‘and said that as a result they were less likely to kill predators’ — the cheetah is protected. In G the dogs protect birds, which are prey, not predators.
  • 28. An optimistic suggestion about possible positive developments → G. Keyword: optimistic. ‘So if we can get this right, there may be a bright future for guard dogs in promoting harmonious coexistence’ — a forward-looking hope, which is what ‘possible developments’ means.
  • 29. How the dogs’ methods keep predators away → B. Keyword: methods. Standing between the livestock and any threat, barking loudly, chasing predators away, or simply being present. Four techniques in two sentences.
  • 30. Claims by different academics that the dogs protect herds successfully → C. Keyword: different academics, plural. Ribeiro in Portugal (90%+ rate performance very good or excellent) and van Bommel and Johnson in Australia (65%+ saw predation stop). Paragraph D has only one academic; paragraph B has a shepherd, not an academic.
  • 31. How the dogs gain their skills → B. Keyword: learn. ‘These dogs are raised from an early age with the animals they will be watching’ plus Macon on ‘their initial training’. Yes, B again — that is exactly why the NB is printed.

Habit to build: when you find your answer for one question, do not cross the paragraph off unless the instructions say each letter is used once. Here, doing so would cost you a mark on Q31.

Skill 2 — Matching Statements to People (Q32–36)

Five people, five statements, each used once. Find the quotation marks before you read the statements — in this passage every answer is inside a direct quote or the sentence introducing one.

  • 32. Guard dogs may save both livestock and wild animals → D (Julie Young). ‘There is common ground from the livestock perspective and from the conservation perspective… If ranchers don’t have a dead cow, they will not… apply for a permit to kill a wolf.’ Two perspectives = both livestock and wildlife.
  • 33. Reported changes in behaviour may not be totally accurate → E (Bethany Smith). ‘up to 88 per cent of farmers said they no longer killed predators — but warned that such self-reported results must be taken with a pinch of salt.’ The behaviour change is ‘no longer killed predators’; the doubt is about the reporting.
  • 34. Negative results if the use is not widespread enough → B (Silvia Ribeiro). ‘In some regions, we work with almost every farmer, but in others only one or two have dogs… If we are not working with everybody, we are transferring the wolf pressure to the neighbour’s herd and he can use poison and kill an entire pack.’
  • 35. The best method, provided the dogs are handled properly → C (Linda van Bommel). ‘If they are managed and used properly, livestock guard dogs are the most efficient control method that we have…’ The conditional clause is the ‘as long as’ in the statement.
  • 36. Training a flock dog needs a different focus from training a house dog → A (Dan Macon). ‘too much human affection makes it a great dog for guarding the front porch, rather than a great livestock guard dog.’ Front porch = house.

Careful: Ribeiro appears twice, in paragraphs C and E, saying opposite kinds of thing. In people-matching tasks always check whether a name is quoted more than once before you commit.

Skill 3 — Summary Completion, ONE WORD ONLY (Q37–40)

Decide the word type for each gap before you look back at the passage. The summary covers paragraph F and the start of G, in order.

  • Q37 jackals — ‘a rise in the deaths of other predators, particularly ______’ needs a plural animal name. Passage: ‘in spite of the impact… on cheetahs, the number of jackals killed by dogs and people actually increased’. ‘Other predators’ rules out cheetahs.
  • Q38 diseases — ‘the dogs could have ______ which may affect other species’ needs a plural noun that a dog can carry. Passage: ‘They have been found to spread diseases to wild animals, including endangered Ethiopian wolves.’
  • Q39 food — ‘reduce the amount of ______’ needs an uncountable noun (because of ‘amount of’). Passage: ‘They may also compete with other carnivores for food.’ Do not write ‘foods’.
  • Q40 foxes — ‘predators such as ______’ needs a plural animal name. Passage: nests ‘in fields, where foxes would normally raid them’.

Grammar as a filter: ‘the amount of’ before Q39 guarantees an uncountable noun, and ‘such as’ before Q40 guarantees an example, almost always plural. The grammar narrows the field before you read a single line of the passage.

Skill 4 — Yes / No / Not Given thinking (extra practice)

These statements are about the writer’s claims, which is what Y/N/NG tests. Decide first, then read the reason.

  • Guard dogs always have to fight predators in order to protect livestock. → NO — ‘often their mere presence is sufficient’, so fighting is not always necessary.
  • The writer fully accepts the 88 per cent figure reported by farmers. → NO — the figure is reported and then immediately qualified with ‘taken with a pinch of salt’.
  • The scale of the harm done to non-target species is well understood. → NO — ‘The extent of this problem isn’t known’.
  • Guard dogs are cheaper than fences for protecting livestock. → NOT GIVEN — the passage calls them ‘the most efficient control method’ but never compares them with fences or mentions cost.
  • The writer believes guard dogs could benefit wildlife in the future. → YES — ‘there may be a bright future for guard dogs in promoting harmonious coexistence between humans and wildlife’.

Rule: if the passage hedges (‘may’, ‘it is possible that’, ‘isn’t known’) then any statement claiming certainty is NO, not NOT GIVEN.

Skill 5 — Multiple choice practice (extra)

MCQ distractors in Passage 3 are built by swapping who said what, or by turning a hedge into a certainty. Try these four.

  • Why did most guard dogs lose their work in the 19th and 20th centuries? → Because predators had been largely exterminated. (Not because farming declined, and not because better methods were invented — neither is mentioned.)
  • What does Dan Macon say is the main aim of a guard dog’s early training? → To make the dog understand that livestock will be its life. (Not to make it aggressive, and not to make it obey humans.)
  • What worries Ribeiro about partial coverage? → That wolves are pushed onto neighbours who may use poison. (Not that the dogs are expensive, and not that wolves learn to avoid dogs.)
  • What did the 2015 Namibian research find? → That jackal deaths rose even though cheetahs benefited. (Not that cheetah numbers fell, and not that the dogs killed no other animals.)

Technique: read the stem, answer it in your own words with the passage covered, then look for the option closest to your own sentence. Choosing between four written options first is how students talk themselves into distractors.

Skill 6 — Locating information fast in a seven-paragraph passage

Passage 3 is long and the clock is tight. Train the jump: idea → paragraph, without re-reading from the top.

  • Why the dogs became unemployed and then needed again → A.
  • How many breeds there are, and how puppies are brought up → B.
  • Survey figures from Portugal and Australia → C.
  • Cheetahs, Namibia, and the ‘permit to kill a wolf’ quote → D.
  • The 88 per cent figure, the pinch of salt, poison and the neighbour’s herd → E.
  • Jackals, diseases, Ethiopian wolves, competition for food, landscape of fear → F.
  • Ground-nesting birds, foxes, biodiversity refuges, bright future → G.

Tip: before answering anything, write two or three words next to each paragraph letter. Ninety seconds spent on that map will save you five minutes of hunting later — and it is the only reliable defence against a passage where one letter is the answer twice.
