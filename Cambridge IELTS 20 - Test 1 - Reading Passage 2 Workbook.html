Reading Passage 2

Cambridge IELTS 20 — Test 1 — Reading Passage 2

Bringing Back the Elm · Matching Information + Matching People + Summary Completion (Q14–26)

📋 Passage Overview

Mark Rowe reports on attempts to reintroduce elm trees to Britain. Around 25 million elms — 90% of the UK total — were killed by Dutch elm disease in the 1960s and ’70s, and the tree has been largely forgotten since. The article explains how the fungus and the elm bark beetle work together, why a few mature trees survived, how important elm timber once was, and whether new disease-resistant hybrids should be planted. Three named experts — Matt Elliot, Karen Russell and Peter Bourne — disagree about the best way forward.

What the passage argues: That elm could return to the British countryside, but only through trees that are resistant to or tolerant of the disease — and that experts genuinely disagree about whether planting part-non-native hybrids is a good idea or whether nature should simply be given time.

段落结构 Structure: A：荷兰榆树病造成的损失与「重新引入」的希望。B：病因——真菌 + 榆小蠹，1970年代第二次疫情源于加拿大进口原木。C：如今榆树只存于矮树篱，树干长到10–15厘米即被攻击；少数百年古树幸存，但样本太少难以研究。D：榆木的历史用途（仅次于橡木）。E：布莱顿的17000棵榆树因海风而幸存，但形势危险。F：抗病苗木的培育与接种测试。G：社会接受度之争——Russell 支持，Elliot 谨慎。

Task type: Q14–18 Matching information to sections A–G (letters may repeat — C is used twice here); Q19–23 Matching statements to people A/B/C; Q24–26 Summary completion, ONE WORD ONLY from the passage.

💡 Reading strategy tip: This passage is lettered A–G, which tells you before you read a word that a matching-information task is coming. Do not read the sections in order: read all five statements (Q14–18) first, note the key noun in each (research problems / difference of opinion / how the disease arrived / a place that escaped / when young elms become vulnerable), then skim for those ideas. For the matching-people task, the fastest method is the opposite: find each name in the passage, read that person’s quotation carefully, and match it to a statement. Every answer in Q19–23 is inside quotation marks — you only need to paraphrase what the person actually said.
⚠️ Traps in this section 本节陷阱:
  • “NB You may use any letter more than once” — five questions but seven sections, and section C is the answer to both Q14 and Q18. Students who assume each letter is used once will force a wrong answer somewhere else.
  • Q16 vs Q18 — both are about the beetle. Q16 wants how the disease entered the country (Canadian log shipments, section B); Q18 wants the size at which a young tree is attacked (10–15 cm trunk, section C). Read the whole statement, not just the topic word.
  • Q19 and Q21 are both Karen Russell — she is quoted three separate times in the passage (sections C and F). Do not stop at her first quotation.
  • Q23 sounds like Peter Bourne because he also describes remembering the disease as a boy. But the statement mentions evidence of how prominent the tree once was, which matches Elliot’s old photographs, not Bourne’s personal memory.
  • Q24–26: ONE WORD ONLY — the passage says ‘storage crates and flooring’, but the summary already gives you ‘boxes’, so the answer is only flooring. Never copy two words when the instruction says one.
  • “ran a close second to oak” — this means oak was first and elm second. Students often read ‘close second’ as if elm were the winner.

🔍 Complex Sentence Analysis

Academic Reading sentences are long because they stack clauses, not because the words are hard. Find the main verb first, then peel off the extra layers. 先找主句谓语动词，再逐层剥离修饰成分。
“Around 25 million elms, accounting for 90% of all elm trees in the UK, died during the 1960s and ’70s of Dutch elm disease.”
Main ClauseAround 25 million elms died during the 1960s and ’70s of Dutch elm disease.
Subordinate / Relative‘accounting for 90% of all elm trees in the UK’ = a participle phrase in commas, adding a statistic about the subject.
Grammar NoteThe subject and its verb are pushed far apart by the comma phrase. Cover everything between the commas and the sentence still works: 25 million elms… died…. Note also the pattern die of + illness.
Simple MeaningDutch elm disease killed about 25 million elms — nine out of every ten in the UK — in the 1960s and 1970s.
中文约2500万棵榆树（占英国榆树总数的90%）在20世纪60至70年代死于荷兰榆树病。
“The wood came in the form of logs destined for boat building and its intact bark was perfect for the elm bark beetles that spread the deadly fungus.”
Main ClauseTwo main clauses: ‘The wood came in the form of logs’ + ‘its intact bark was perfect for the elm bark beetles’.
Subordinate / Relative‘destined for boat building’ = a reduced relative (which were destined for…); ‘that spread the deadly fungus’ = a defining relative clause describing the beetles.
Grammar NoteTwo different reduction techniques in one sentence. Learn to see noun + past participle (logs destined) as a hidden relative clause.
Simple MeaningThe imported wood was logs for building boats, and because the bark was undamaged the beetles that carry the fungus could live in it.
中文这些木材以准备用于造船的原木形式运来，其完好的树皮正好适合传播致命真菌的榆小蠹寄生。
“Yet mature specimens have been identified, in counties such as Cambridgeshire, that are hundreds of years old, and have mysteriously escaped the epidemic.”
Main ClauseMature specimens have been identified.
Subordinate / Relative‘that are hundreds of years old, and have mysteriously escaped the epidemic’ = a relative clause separated from its noun by the phrase ‘in counties such as Cambridgeshire’.
Grammar NoteThe relative clause does not describe Cambridgeshire — it describes specimens. When a relative clause seems to modify the wrong noun, check whether a phrase has been inserted in front of it.
Simple MeaningSome very old elms, for example in Cambridgeshire, have been found alive, and nobody knows why they were not killed.
中文然而在剑桥郡等地已发现一些树龄达数百年的成熟个体，它们不知何故躲过了这场疫情。
“The key, Russell says, is to identify and study those trees that have survived and work out why they stood tall when millions of others succumbed.”
Main ClauseThe key is to identify and study those trees… and (to) work out why…
Subordinate / Relative‘Russell says’ = a reporting clause dropped into the middle; ‘that have survived’ = defining relative; ‘why they stood tall when millions of others succumbed’ = an embedded question.
Grammar NoteTwo infinitives share one ‘to’ (to identify and study… and work out). The inserted ‘Russell says’ is your signal that everything around it can be matched to her in Q19–23.
Simple MeaningRussell believes the answer is to find the surviving trees and discover why they lived when millions of others died.
中文Russell 认为，关键在于找出并研究那些幸存的树木，弄清为何在数百万棵树死去时它们仍屹立不倒。
“This means a widespread reintroduction would involve existing or new hybrid strains derived from resistant, generally non-native elm species.”
Main ClauseThis means (that) a widespread reintroduction would involve existing or new hybrid strains.
Subordinate / Relative‘derived from resistant, generally non-native elm species’ = a reduced relative describing the strains.
Grammar NoteThe conditional would shows this is a plan, not a fact. ‘Generally non-native’ is the detail that creates the argument in section G.
Simple MeaningBringing elms back on a large scale would mean planting hybrids bred from disease-resistant elms that usually come from other countries.
中文这意味着大规模重新引入将涉及由具抗性的、通常并非本土的榆树种培育出的现有或新的杂交品系。
“Rather than plant new elms, the Woodland Trust emphasises providing space to those elms that have survived independently.”
Main ClauseThe Woodland Trust emphasises providing space to those elms.
Subordinate / Relative‘Rather than plant new elms’ = a fronted phrase of preference; ‘that have survived independently’ = defining relative clause.
Grammar NoteRather than + bare infinitive sets up a contrast between two policies. This sentence is the core of Q15 (a difference of opinion) and Q20 (Elliot’s view).
Simple MeaningInstead of planting new trees, the Woodland Trust wants to give room to the elms that survived on their own.
中文林地信托强调的不是种植新榆树，而是为那些自行存活下来的榆树留出生长空间。

🧠 Reading Logic Training

IELTS Reading tests whether you follow the relationship between ideas: cause, contrast, example, definition, sequence and the writer’s own attitude. 抓住句子之间的逻辑关系，比逐词翻译更重要。

Cause & effect — two agents, one disease

Follow the chain in section B: a fungus blocks the vascular system → branches wilt and die; Canadian logs arrive with intact bark → elm bark beetles survive the journey → the beetles spread a more virulent strain → most British elms die. The beetle is the carrier, the fungus is the killer. Q16 asks how the disease was brought in, so the answer must be the shipment, not the beetle in general.

Contrast — ‘Yet’, ‘However’, ‘Nevertheless’

Each of these words marks a place where the passage turns, and IELTS loves to build questions there. “Yet mature specimens have been identified…” (survivors exist); “Nevertheless, opportunities are limited…” (but too few to study — Q14); “However, the situation is precarious” (Brighton is not safe — Q22).

Comparison & ranking — where elm actually stood

“elm ran a close second to oak as the hardwood tree of choice” ranks the two trees, and the summary in Q24 turns that ranking round: the only tree that was more popular than elm was…. When a question reverses a comparison, translate it into a simple order first: 1. oak, 2. elm.

Examples — ‘such as’ and named cases

“in counties such as Cambridgeshire”, “items such as storage crates and flooring”, “the 19th-century sailing ship Cutty Sark”. Concrete examples are the easiest text to scan for, which is why summary-completion answers (Q25, Q26) so often sit inside them.

Definition — brackets, dashes and footnotes

The passage defines its own hard words: ‘vascular (water, nutrient and food transport) system’ and the footnote explaining horticultural analogue as a cultivated species genetically similar to an existing one. Read footnotes before you read the questions — here the footnote is what makes Elliot’s objection (‘You’re effectively cloning’) understandable.

Problem → solution → objection

Problem: 90% of elms died. Proposed solution: plant resistant or tolerant hybrids (section F). Objection: they are partly non-native, they are effectively clones, and they may carry new diseases (section G). Alternative solution: give the natural survivors space and time. Q15 asks for the section where the disagreement is, which is exactly this last stage — section G.

Chronology — a timeline hidden in a non-chronological article

Bronze Age (tools, shields) → 18th century (wider planting, crates, flooring) → 19th century (Cutty Sark’s keel) → 1920s (first epidemic, dies down) → 1960s–70s (second epidemic, 25 million dead) → today (17,000 elms in Brighton; new seedlings tested). Section D holds the old dates, sections A–B the modern ones.

Attitude — three named voices, three positions

Karen Russell: scientific and pro-reintroduction (‘the environmental case… is strong’). Matt Elliot: cautious, prefers natural recovery (‘Others are more wary’, ‘On the face of it, it seems like a good idea’ — a phrase that always introduces a but). Peter Bourne: personal and protective about Brighton. Sorting the speakers by attitude solves Q19–23 faster than re-reading each quotation.

🎯 Question-Type Skill Practice

Skill 1 — Matching information to sections (Q14–18)

The instruction says a letter may be used more than once — and here section C is used twice. Work statement by statement, and stop as soon as you find the idea.

  • 14. research problems caused by there being few large survivors → C — ‘opportunities are limited as the number of these mature survivors is relatively small’.
  • 15. a difference of opinion about the value of reintroduction → G — Russell says the environmental case is strong; ‘Others are more wary’ and Elliot objects.
  • 16. how the disease was brought into Britain → B — the second epidemic ‘was triggered by shipments of elm from Canada’.
  • 17. conditions that let one location escape the disease → E — Brighton’s strong sea winds make it hard for the beetle to attack.
  • 18. the stage at which young elms become vulnerable → C — once the trunk reaches 10–15 cm in diameter it is perfect for beetles and fungus.

Method: convert each statement into two or three keywords before you search. For Q17 that is place + escaped; scanning for ‘Brighton’ and ‘winds’ takes seconds, whereas reading section E line by line takes minutes.

Skill 2 — Matching statements to people (Q19–23)

Every answer is inside a quotation. Find the name, read what the person said, then paraphrase it.

  • 19. damage becomes visible quickly → B (Karen Russell) — ‘The effects are very quick… You return in four to six weeks’.
  • 20. better to wait and see whether surviving elms flourish → A (Matt Elliot) — ‘Sometimes the best thing you can do is just give nature time to recover’.
  • 21. there must be an explanation for some elms surviving → B (Karen Russell) — ‘I don’t see how it can be entirely down to luck’.
  • 22. disease-carrying insects are close by → C (Peter Bourne) — ‘the threat is right on our doorstep’.
  • 23. you grasp the impact when you see how prominent the tree was → A (Matt Elliot) — ‘You look at old photographs from the 1960s and it’s only then that you realise the impact’.

Warning: two answers are Russell and two are Elliot. If your five answers are all different letters in a task with only three names, you have almost certainly made a mistake.

Skill 3 — Summary completion, ONE WORD ONLY (Q24–26)

The summary follows one section only (D). Find that section first, then read the summary sentence by sentence.

  • Q24 oak — ‘elm ran a close second to oak’, so oak was the more popular tree. The gap needs a tree name, singular, no article.
  • Q25 flooring — the passage pairs ‘storage crates and flooring’; the summary has already used ‘boxes’ for crates, so only flooring is left.
  • Q26 keel — ‘used to build the keel of the 19th-century sailing ship Cutty Sark’. Write the single noun.

Grammar check: Q26 reads ‘the Cutty Sark’s ______ was also constructed’. The singular verb was tells you the answer is a singular noun — a free clue that costs you nothing to use.

Skill 4 — True / False / Not Given practice (extra)

Same passage, different task type. Decide first, then read the reason.

  • The 1920s epidemic killed more elms than the 1970s one. → FALSE — the first epidemic ‘gradually died down’; the second destroyed the vast majority.
  • Elms in hedgerows are attacked only after they reach a certain size. → TRUE — ‘they get targeted by the beetle as soon as they reach a certain size’.
  • Brighton has more elms than any other British city. → NOT GIVEN — the figure 17,000 is given, but no comparison with other cities is made.
  • Scientists know exactly why some mature elms survived. → FALSE — ‘We don’t know where the balance lies between the three.’
  • The resistance test involves injecting the pathogen into the bark. → TRUE — ‘cutting a small slit on the bark and injecting a tiny amount of the pathogen’.

Skill 5 — Matching headings practice (extra)

Which heading fits each section? A heading must cover the whole section, not one striking detail.

  • Section B → How the disease works and how it arrived.
  • Section C → Survivors, and why they are hard to study — not ‘Elms in hedgerows’, which is only the opening detail.
  • Section D → A timber with a long history of use.
  • Section E → One city where the tree survived — for now.
  • Section F → Testing a new generation of trees.
  • Section G → Should we plant them at all?

Watch out: sections C and E both mention trees that escaped the disease. C is about research on survivors; E is about one place and why it was lucky. Distinguish headings by purpose, not by topic.

Skill 6 — Multiple choice practice (extra)

Choose the best option, then check the reasoning. In MCQ, three options are usually true statements about the passage that simply do not answer the question asked.

  • Why was the second epidemic so much worse than the first? A. More elms had been planted. B. The beetles carried a more virulent strain. C. Nobody tried to stop it. D. The winters were milder. → B, stated directly in section B.
  • What does Elliot object to about hybrid elms? A. They cost too much. B. They grow too slowly. C. They are effectively clones of a native species. D. They attract more beetles. → C — ‘You’re replacing a native species with a horticultural analogue… You’re effectively cloning.’
  • How is a seedling’s resistance tested? A. By planting it beside a diseased tree. B. By measuring its trunk. C. By injecting the pathogen through a slit in the bark. D. By counting its leaves in autumn. → C, section F.

Review Quiz

Q1
C. about 90%
‘Around 25 million elms, accounting for 90% of all elm trees in the UK, died…’ The figure 25 million is the number, not the percentage.
Q2
A. a fungus that blocks the vascular system
The beetle is only the carrier: ‘The disease is caused by a fungus that blocks the elms’ vascular… system, causing branches to wilt and die.’
Q3
B. through shipments of elm logs from Canada
Section B: the logs were ‘destined for boat building and its intact bark was perfect for the elm bark beetles’. The boats are what the wood was for, not how it travelled.
Q4
C. when its trunk reaches 10–15 cm in diameter
Section C: at that size the trunk ‘becomes a perfect size for beetles to lay eggs and for the fungus to take hold’.
Q5
C. There are relatively few of them.
‘Nevertheless, opportunities are limited as the number of these mature survivors is relatively small.’ The word ‘Nevertheless’ marks the problem.
Q6
A. avoidance, tolerance and resistance
‘Avoidance, tolerance, resistance? We don’t know where the balance lies between the three.’ She then rejects pure luck — the basis of Q21.
Q7
B. Oak was first and elm a close second.
‘elm ran a close second to oak as the hardwood tree of choice’ — the sentence behind Q24 (oak).
Q8
C. Strong sea winds make it hard for the beetle to attack.
Section E: ‘Strong winds from the sea make it difficult for the determined elm bark beetle to attack this coastal city’s elm population.’
Q9
B. The danger is very close and could easily arrive.
He says ‘The beetles can just march in if we’re not careful’ — a warning, not a report that they have arrived. The passage calls the situation ‘precarious’.
Q10
B. A small amount of the pathogen is injected through a slit in the bark.
Section F. Russell adds ‘The effects are very quick… four to six weeks’ — the evidence for Q19.
Q11
C. It replaces a native species with a genetically similar cultivated one.
‘You’re replacing a native species with a horticultural analogue… You’re effectively cloning.’ He also mentions the risk of new diseases.
Q12
A. ‘On the face of it, it seems like a good idea’
‘On the face of it’ always introduces a but. Recognising these hedging phrases lets you predict the writer’s next move.
