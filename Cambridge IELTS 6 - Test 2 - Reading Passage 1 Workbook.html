Advantages of Public Transport  |  Practice Workbook

📋 Passage Overview

This passage, titled "Advantages of Public Transport", presents findings from a World Bank study conducted by Murdoch University's Institute for Science and Technology Policy (ISTP). The study compared how much wealth thirty-seven cities around the world spent on transport, and found that cities with good public transport systems are far more economically efficient than car-dependent cities. Professor Peter Newman, the ISTP Director, argues that the traditional case for public transport — based on environmental and social fairness — should now also include a strong economic argument.

The passage examines specific cities as case studies (Perth, Melbourne, Portland, Stockholm, Amsterdam, Auckland) and responds to common objections against public transport, such as climate and geography. It ends by discussing how telecommunications, urban design (the "Urban Village" concept), and the clustering of creative workers all support the continued growth and density of cities.

💡 Reading strategy tip: This passage uses the matching headings, True/False/Not Given, and matching features question types. For headings, focus on the main idea of each paragraph — not the first sentence alone. For T/F/NG, map each statement to a specific sentence in the text and check very carefully whether the text contradicts it (FALSE) or simply doesn't mention it (NOT GIVEN).

🔍 Complex Sentence Analysis

These sentences from the passage contain advanced grammar. Study how each one is built, then check the simple meaning and Chinese translation.

"A new study conducted for the World Bank by Murdoch University's Institute for Science and Technology Policy (ISTP) has demonstrated that public transport is more efficient than cars."
Main Clause A new study … has demonstrated that public transport is more efficient than cars.
Reduced Relative Clause conducted for the World Bank by Murdoch University's ISTP — a past participial phrase describing the study (= "which was conducted…")
Grammar Note Past participle used as a reduced (shortened) relative clause. Also note the present perfect "has demonstrated" — the finding is relevant now.
Simple Meaning A study done for the World Bank by an Australian university has shown that public transport beats private cars.
中文翻译 由默多克大学科学技术政策研究所为世界银行开展的一项新研究表明，公共交通比汽车更高效。
"Newman says this is a new, broader way of considering public transport issues. In the past, the case for public transport has been made on the basis of environmental and social justice considerations rather than economics."
Main Clause The case for public transport has been made on the basis of environmental and social justice considerations.
Contrast Structure rather than economics — contrasts the old justification (environment/fairness) with the new one (economics)
Grammar Note Present perfect passive: "has been made" = someone made the argument (focus on the argument, not the person). "rather than" = instead of, not.
Simple Meaning In the past, people argued for public transport because it is good for the environment and fair to all — not for money reasons. Newman says we should also use economic arguments.
中文翻译 过去，支持公共交通的理由是基于环境保护和社会公正的考量，而非经济因素。纽曼认为这是一种考量公共交通问题的更宏观的新方式。
"There is a widespread belief that increasing wealth encourages people to live farther out where cars are the only viable transport."
Main Structure There is a widespread belief — existential "there is" introduces a commonly-held idea
That-Clause that increasing wealth encourages people to live farther out — the content of the belief
Relative Clause where cars are the only viable transport — describes "farther out" (the outer suburbs)
Grammar Note "There is a belief that…" is a hedging structure — the writer reports an idea without agreeing with it. The passage then refutes this belief.
Simple Meaning Many people think that as people get richer, they move to the outer suburbs where they need a car. (But the passage argues this is wrong.)
中文翻译 人们普遍认为，随着财富增加，人们会倾向于住得更远，而那些地方只有汽车才是唯一可行的交通方式。
"In cities that developed later, the World Bank and Asian Development Bank discouraged the building of public transport and people have been forced to rely on cars — creating the massive traffic jams that characterize those cities."
Main Clause the World Bank and Asian Development Bank discouraged the building of public transport and people have been forced to rely on cars
Relative Clause that developed later — refers to cities like Jakarta and Bangkok
Participial Phrase (Result) creating the massive traffic jams that characterize those cities — the result of forcing car use
Grammar Note A present participle phrase after a dash (—) often shows the result or consequence of the main clause.
Simple Meaning In newer cities like Jakarta and Bangkok, international banks stopped them building public transport, so people had to use cars — which caused the terrible traffic jams those cities are famous for.
中文翻译 在发展较晚的城市中，世界银行和亚洲开发银行不鼓励建设公共交通，迫使人们依赖汽车出行——由此造成了这些城市标志性的严重交通堵塞。
"It was once assumed that improvements in telecommunications would lead to more dispersal in the population as people were no longer forced into cities."
Main Structure It was once assumed that… — impersonal passive reporting a past assumption (now proved wrong)
That-Clause that improvements in telecommunications would lead to more dispersal in the population
As-Clause (Reason) as people were no longer forced into cities — explains why dispersal was expected
Grammar Note "It was assumed that…" = impersonal passive used to report a general idea without naming who believed it. "Once" signals the belief is now outdated. "Would" = past form of future prediction.
Simple Meaning People used to think that better technology (phones, internet) would spread people away from cities, since they no longer needed to live close together to work. (But research showed the opposite happened.)
中文翻译 人们曾经认为，电信技术的进步会导致人口更加分散，因为人们不再需要被迫聚集在城市里。

🧠 Reading Logic Training

Academic passages use different logical patterns to organise information. Recognising these patterns helps you answer questions faster. Here are the key patterns in this passage.

⚡ Cause & Effect

Cause: Cities relied on the car-dependent model; the World Bank discouraged public transport in developing Asian cities.

Effect: Massive traffic jams now characterise cities like Jakarta and Bangkok.

Signal words in the passage: "creating the massive traffic jams", "causing massive congestion problems"

因果关系信号词：causing, leading to, as a result, resulting in, creating…

⚖️ Contrast

Old view vs. New view: Public transport was defended on environmental/social grounds; Newman argues for economic grounds.

Wealthy → more cars? No: European cities are wealthier than American ones but use far fewer cars. Stockholm's car use fell as it became richer.

Signal words: "on the other hand", "however", "rather than", "but"

对比信号词：however, on the other hand, in contrast, whereas, rather than…

📊 Comparison

Perth vs. European/Asian cities: Perth spends 17% of its wealth on transport; efficient cities spend as little as 5%.

Portland vs. Perth: Both had similar population and density — but Portland chose light rail and transformed its city.

Signal words: "compared to", "as little as", "the same … as"

比较信号词：compared to/with, similarly, likewise, in the same way…

💡 Example

Purpose: The passage uses specific city examples to prove or disprove general claims.

Portland → proves that politics (democracy) drives transport choice

Amsterdam & Copenhagen → proves that bicycle-friendly cities can be efficient even without outstanding public transport

Hong Kong & Zürich → proves that topography (hills) is not a true barrier to rail

举例信号词：for example, for instance, such as, as in the case of…

🔧 Problem → Solution

Problem: Car-dependent cities grow too large; pushing everyone into the city centre doesn't work.

Solution (proposed): The Urban Village report advocated creating urban villages at hundreds of sites around railway stations — decentralising density without creating sprawl.

问题-解决信号词：the problem is…, one solution is…, this can be addressed by…

🎙️ Author's Attitude

The author presents Newman's views positively, using words like "demonstrated" and "pointed out" (factual, verified) rather than "claimed" or "argued" (uncertain).

Key evaluative language revealing Newman's stance: "inefficient and grossly inadequate" (about the car-dependent model) and "spectacular" (about Portland's rail success).

判断作者态度：注意评价性词语（positive/negative），情态动词（should, must, might），以及引用动词（demonstrate vs. claim vs. suggest）

🔄 Concession → Counter-argument

The passage addresses objections (concession) then counters them:

Objection 1 — Climate: "Some people say their city could not make more use of public transport because it is either too hot or too cold."
Counter: Newman found "zero correlation" between climate and car use; Toronto and Singapore both have successful public transport.

Objection 2 — Topography: "Newman accepts it would be hard for a city as hilly as Auckland…"
Counter: Hong Kong and Zürich have made their rail systems work despite being hilly.

让步-反驳结构信号词：admittedly, although, while it is true that… / however, nevertheless, yet, but in fact…

✅ Answer Key

Questions 1–5 — Matching Headings

Q1 (Para A)
ii — A successful exercise in people power
Para A is about the Portland referendum where local people voted to spend money on light rail instead of roads — this is the "people power" (democratic process) that Newman favours.
Q2 (Para B)
vii — Increases in travelling time
Para B says UK commuting times were stable for centuries, but urban sprawl caused congestion, making commuting times "far higher" — i.e., travel times increased.
Q3 (Para C)
iv — Higher incomes need not mean more cars
Para C directly challenges the belief that wealth leads to car use, using Stockholm (car use fell as the city got richer) and European vs. American cities as evidence.
Q4 (Para D)
i — Avoiding an overcrowded centre
Para D describes The Urban Village report, which argues against pushing everyone into the city centre. The solution is distributed urban villages around stations.
Q5 (Para E)
vi — The impact of telecommunications on population distribution
Para E directly discusses the (failed) prediction that telecommunications would disperse population away from cities. Research showed city density actually rose.
