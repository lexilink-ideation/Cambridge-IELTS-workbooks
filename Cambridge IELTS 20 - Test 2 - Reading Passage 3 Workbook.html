Reading Passage 3

Cambridge IELTS 20 — Test 2 — Reading Passage 3

Invasion of the Robot Umpires · Yes / No / Not Given + Summary (List of Phrases) + Multiple Choice (Q27–40)

📋 Passage Overview

This passage is a piece of journalism with an argument inside it. It describes how Major League Baseball began replacing the human umpire’s judgment with the Automated Ball-Strike System (ABS), nicknamed the ‘robo-umpire’, and reports what officials, coaches, a philosopher and a player think about it. The writer is not neutral: after presenting MLB’s case for accuracy, he keeps returning to one question — if the arguing stops, has the game lost something human? Because it is an opinion piece, the True/False task becomes YES / NO / NOT GIVEN: you are judging the writer’s claims, not the facts of baseball.

What the passage argues: That ABS really is more accurate than a human umpire — and that this may not be the point. Arguments over the strike zone have been ‘the game’s animating force’ for 150 years, and the writer repeatedly suggests that removing controversy may also remove some of the pleasure, because sport is one of the places where imperfection is enjoyable rather than a fault.

段落结构 Structure: 第1–2段：DeJesus 首次使用 ABS，好球区的定义，全场鸦雀无声（对应 Q33–37 的摘要题）。第3段：150年来关于好球区的争吵，以及机器来了之后争吵消失。第4段：首季怪判 → MLB 微调尺寸 → 一致认为极其稳定；Manfred 说能减少争议；作者反问争议是否值得减少（Q28、Q29）。第5段：教练 Viola 批评 ABS 不留情面、迂腐、死抠条文，怀念投手与裁判之间的默契（Q38）。第6段：Sword 解释动机——怕失去年轻观众；1960年代快速球让比赛变得单调（Q30、Q39）。第7段：各种改革方案都被排除，只有 ABS 是理想载体，好球区可以是任何形状（Q31、Q32）。第8段：与 VAR、鹰眼的对比——那些判的是有形的界线。第9–10段：哲学家 Noe 与球员 Russo 的反对意见（Q40）。

Task type: Q27–32 Yes / No / Not Given (the writer’s claims, in passage order); Q33–37 Summary completion from a list of phrases A–H (eight options, five gaps, so three options are never used); Q38–40 Multiple choice, four options — one about a named paragraph, one about a named speaker, one about the writer’s purpose.

💡 Reading strategy tip: Three task types sit on one passage, and they are not equally expensive. Do the summary (Q33–37) first: it covers only paragraphs 1–3, the phrases are given to you, and grammar alone eliminates most options. Then do Y/N/NG (Q27–32), which runs in passage order across the whole text. Leave the three multiple-choice questions (Q38–40) until last, because two of them ask about the writer’s purpose and you answer those far better once you have read everything. For Y/N/NG, remember the split: NO means the writer says the opposite; NOT GIVEN means the topic may appear but the specific claim never does. Four of the six statements in this set hang on that difference.
⚠️ Traps in this section 本节陷阱:
  • Q29 — money is never mentioned. The paragraph is full of precision (‘near-perfect’, ‘fractions of an inch’) and MLB clearly paid for the system, so the statement feels covered. But ‘justify the money spent’ appears nowhere → NOT GIVEN.
  • Q31 — the bats really are there. ‘from changing the bats to changing the geometry of the field’ makes you feel you have found the answer. What is missing is the fierce debate: the passage only says the ideas were discussed and ruled out → NOT GIVEN.
  • Q30 — ‘more exciting’ belongs to the wrong noun. The phrase ‘make baseball more exciting’ describes Sword’s project, not the fastball. The fastball ‘flattened the game… a type of play lacking much action’ → NO.
  • Q37 — silence is not approval. Option E (widespread approval) is the trap for option G (total silence). ‘There was no heckling and no shouted disagreement. Nobody said a word’ tells you only that people were quiet — the passage never says they were pleased.
  • Q35 — ‘boundary’ is borrowed from the wrong paragraph. Option A (pitch boundary) uses the word boundaries that appears in paragraph 3 about arguments. The gap needs the definition in paragraph 2: an imaginary zone → H, perceived area.
  • Q38 says ‘the fifth paragraph’. Count the paragraphs before you read. Paragraph 5 begins ‘A human, at least, yells back’ — the Viola paragraph. Answering from paragraph 4 (Manfred) gives you the opposite view.

🔍 Complex Sentence Analysis

Academic Reading sentences are long because they stack clauses, not because the words are hard. Find the main verb first, then peel off the extra layers. 先找主句谓语动词，再逐层剥离修饰成分。
“Instead of making any judgments himself about a strike, DeJesus had decisions fed to him through an earpiece, connected to a modified missile-tracking system.”
Main ClauseDeJesus had decisions fed to him through an earpiece.
Subordinate / Relative‘Instead of making any judgments himself’ = a preposition + -ing phrase of replacement; ‘connected to a modified missile-tracking system’ = a reduced relative clause describing the earpiece.
Grammar Notehave + object + past participle (‘had decisions fed to him’) means someone else does it to you. He is the receiver, not the doer — which is exactly what Q27 tests.
Simple MeaningDeJesus did not decide anything himself; a machine told him what to say.
中文德赫苏斯并不自己判断好球与否，而是通过耳机接收判罚结果，耳机连接着一套改装过的导弹追踪系统。
“Even if the batter does not hit the ball, a pitch that passes through the strike zone is considered a strike.”
Main ClauseA pitch is considered a strike.
Subordinate / Relative‘Even if the batter does not hit the ball’ = a concession clause; ‘that passes through the strike zone’ = a defining relative clause narrowing which pitch.
Grammar NoteEven if introduces a condition that does not change the result. The passive ‘is considered’ is how rules and definitions are usually written in English.
Simple MeaningA ball that crosses the zone counts as a strike whether or not the batter swings at it.
中文即使击球手没有击中球，只要投球穿过好球区，就算作一次好球。
“But the question is whether controversy is worth reducing, or whether it is the sign of a human hand.”
Main ClauseThe question is whether controversy is worth reducing.
Subordinate / Relative‘or whether it is the sign of a human hand’ = a second noun clause sharing the same verb ‘is’.
Grammar Notewhether A or whether B sets up a genuine alternative. ‘be worth + -ing’ takes no object after the -ing form. This single sentence is the writer’s thesis.
Simple MeaningShould we really get rid of arguing — or is arguing proof that humans are involved?
中文但问题在于：争议究竟值不值得被减少，还是说它恰恰是“人的痕迹”？
“According to Sword, ABS was part of a larger project to make baseball more exciting since executives are terrified of losing younger fans, as has been the case with horse racing and boxing.”
Main ClauseABS was part of a larger project.
Subordinate / Relative‘to make baseball more exciting’ = infinitive of purpose; ‘since executives are terrified…’ = a reason clause; ‘as has been the case with…’ = a comparison clause with no subject of its own.
Grammar Notesince here means because, not from that time. Three layers of information hang off one short main clause — find the main verb (was) first.
Simple MeaningSword says ABS was introduced because bosses fear young people will stop watching baseball, the way they stopped watching horse racing and boxing.
中文斯沃德表示，ABS 只是让棒球更精彩这一更大计划的一部分，因为高管们非常害怕失去年轻观众——赛马和拳击就是前车之鉴。
“This type of baseball essentially hasn’t existed since the 1960s, when the hundred-mile-an-hour fastball, which is difficult to hit and control, entered the game.”
Main ClauseThis type of baseball hasn’t existed since the 1960s.
Subordinate / Relative‘when the… fastball… entered the game’ = a relative clause of time; ‘which is difficult to hit and control’ = a non-defining relative clause nested inside it.
Grammar NotePresent perfect + since means the situation has continued up to now. Notice the clause inside a clause: the subject (fastball) and its verb (entered) are pushed seven words apart.
Simple MeaningExciting, action-filled baseball has been missing since the 1960s, when very fast pitching arrived.
中文这种类型的棒球自20世纪60年代起基本上就不存在了——当时时速百英里、既难打中又难控制的快速球进入了比赛。
“Some players I have met take a dissenting stance toward the robots too, believing that accuracy is not the answer.”
Main ClauseSome players take a dissenting stance toward the robots.
Subordinate / Relative‘I have met’ = a contact relative clause with that/whom omitted; ‘believing that accuracy is not the answer’ = a present-participle clause giving their reason.
Grammar NoteEnglish lets you drop the object relative pronoun: players (whom) I have met. The word too links these players back to Noe — the grouping Q40 asks about.
Simple MeaningSome players I know also disagree with the robots, because they think being accurate is not what matters.
中文我见过的一些球员同样对机器人持反对立场，他们认为准确并不是问题的答案。

🧠 Reading Logic Training

IELTS Reading tests whether you follow the relationship between ideas: cause, contrast, example, definition, sequence and the writer’s own attitude. 抓住句子之间的逻辑关系，比逐词翻译更重要。

Cause & effect — two chains running in opposite directions

Chain one is technological: players complain about strange calls → MLB tweaks the dimensions → the consensus becomes ‘profoundly consistent’. Chain two is human: the robot arrives → “the arguments stopped” → something the writer values disappears. Q28 lives on the first chain (complaint causes the change, so YES); Q38 lives on the second.

Contrast — the signal words that carry the argument

Watch Instead of (paragraph 1: he does not judge — Q27), Previously (paragraph 2: the job used to be a judgment call — Q34), however (paragraph 3: “When the robo-umpires came, however, the arguments stopped”) and above all But in paragraph 4: “But the question is whether controversy is worth reducing…”. In an opinion piece, the sentence after ‘but’ is usually the writer’s own view.

Comparison & analogy — why ABS is not the same as VAR or Hawk-Eye

Paragraph 8 compares four technologies and then draws a line through them: soccer’s VAR, tennis’s Hawk-Eye and baseball’s instant replay all judge physical things — bases, lines, goals, “delineated like the keys of a piano”. ABS judges an imaginary box. The comparison is the argument: precision is fair when the boundary is real, and questionable when it is not.

Examples — what they are doing in each paragraph

‘Horse racing and boxing’ are examples of sports that lost young audiences (they support Q39). ‘A triangle, or a blob, or something shaped like Texas’ are examples of what ABS makes possible (they support Q32). ‘Changing the bats… changing the geometry of the field’ are examples of ideas that were rejected (Q31). Ask every example: is it evidence for, or evidence against?

Definition & apposition — information tucked inside brackets, commas and dashes

The strike zone is defined in brackets as “an imaginary zone about seventeen inches wide, stretching from the batter’s knees to the middle of his chest” — that bracket alone answers Q35. Apposition also identifies every speaker: ‘Rob Manfred, who is Commissioner for MLB’, ‘Frank Viola, a coach for a North Carolina team’, ‘Alva Noe, a professor at Berkeley University and a baseball fan’. Build a quick who-is-who list as you read.

Problem → solution — the shape of paragraphs 6 and 7

Problem: young fans are being lost, and fans say they want more action. Investigation: ask the fans, then brainstorm every rule that could change. Rejection: all the obvious fixes are ruled out. Solution: ABS, because any zone can be loaded into it. Notice that the solution is presented in Sword’s voice, not the writer’s — which is why Q39 asks what Sword says.

Chronology — the time markers that let you locate answers fast

‘A few years ago’ (DeJesus, paragraph 1) → ‘For a hundred and fifty years or so’ (the tradition of arguing, paragraph 3) → ‘During the first robo-umpire season… the following year’ (complaints, then the tweak, paragraph 4) → ‘since the 1960s’ (the fastball, paragraph 6) → ‘For almost a decade… In the past twenty years’ (other sports, paragraph 8). Two of the six Y/N/NG answers sit next to a time marker.

The writer’s attitude — reported, then revealed

For four paragraphs the writer only reports. Then he starts to appear: a rhetorical question (“But the question is whether controversy is worth reducing”), a concession (“Perhaps he’s right”), and finally a direct claim in his own voice (“We watch baseball to kill time, not to maximize it”). In Y/N/NG and purpose questions, these first-person moments are worth more than any quotation.

🎯 Question-Type Skill Practice

Skill 1 — YES / NO / NOT GIVEN (Q27–32)

YES = the writer claims this. NO = the writer claims the opposite. NOT GIVEN = the writer never makes this claim, even if the topic appears. Decide first, then read the reason.

  • 27. DeJesus shared decision-making about strikes with ABS. → NO — ‘Instead of making any judgments himself… DeJesus had decisions fed to him’. He announced; he did not share. The statement says the opposite.
  • 28. MLB thought it necessary to change the size of the zone after player criticism. → YES — ‘players complained about some strange calls. In response, MLB decided to tweak the dimensions’. Complaint + response = exactly the claim.
  • 29. MLB is keen to justify the money spent on improving ABS’s accuracy. → NOT GIVEN — MLB commissioned the system and boasts about its precision, but cost, value for money and justification are never mentioned anywhere in the passage.
  • 30. The hundred-mile-an-hour fastball led to a more exciting style of play. → NO — it ‘flattened the game into strikeouts, walks, and home runs — a type of play lacking much action’.
  • 31. The differing proposals for changing the bat led to fierce debate on Sword’s team. → NOT GIVEN — bats are mentioned in a list of things they ‘talked about changing’, and all the ideas were ruled out. No disagreement, and certainly no fierceness, is described.
  • 32. ABS makes changes to the shape of the strike zone feasible. → YES — ‘you can load any strike zone you want into the system. It might be a triangle, or a blob…’ Feasible = possible.

Habit to build: circle the one word carrying the risk — shared, necessary, justify, more exciting, fierce, feasible. That word, not the topic, decides the answer. And when a statement adds an emotion (fierce, keen, reluctant) that the passage never reports, NOT GIVEN is very likely.

Skill 2 — Summary Completion from a list of phrases (Q33–37)

Eight options, five gaps: three are pure distractors. Work grammar first, then meaning, then the paragraph. This summary only covers paragraphs 1–3.

Step 1 — sort the options by grammar. Countable plural: numerous disputes, team tactics, former roles. Countable singular after ‘a’: subjective assessment, perceived area, pitch boundary. Uncountable: widespread approval, total silence. Now look at the gaps: Q34 and Q35 both follow ‘a’, so only three options can fill each; Q36 is the subject of ‘were not uncommon’, so it must be plural; Q37 follows ‘met with’, which takes an uncountable reaction.

  • 33 = F, former roles — ‘announce the calls, just as they would have done in the past’. ‘As they had in their ___’ needs a plural noun about the job they used to do.
  • 34 = D, subjective assessment — ‘calling a strike was a judgment call on the part of the umpire’. Judgment call = subjective assessment; ‘at one time’ matches ‘Previously’.
  • 35 = H, perceived area — ‘an imaginary zone about seventeen inches wide’. Imaginary = perceived (it exists only in the mind). A ‘pitch boundary’ would be a line you can see.
  • 36 = B, numerous disputes — ‘countless arguments… have taken place over its boundaries’. Countless = numerous, arguments = disputes, and the verb ‘were’ confirms the plural.
  • 37 = G, total silence — ‘there was no heckling and no shouted disagreement. Nobody said a word.’

The three unused options tell a story too: C (team tactics) is off-topic and appears nowhere; A (pitch boundary) steals the word boundaries from the wrong paragraph; E (widespread approval) turns silence into agreement. Distractors are almost always built from a real word in a wrong place, or a real situation given a wrong emotion.

Skill 3 — Multiple choice (Q38–40): three different kinds of question

Q38 is located in a named paragraph, Q39 belongs to a named person, and Q40 asks about the writer’s purpose. Each needs a different search.

  • 38. What does the writer suggest about ABS in the fifth paragraph? → B (it may reduce some of the appeal of the game). Paragraph 5 is the Viola paragraph: the system is ‘unforgiving and pedantic, almost legalistic’, its zone is ‘too precise’, and the old ‘dialogue between pitcher and umpire’ has gone. That is a loss of pleasure, not of correctness. A is wrong because the passage insists the calls are right; C (human umpires disappearing) is never suggested — DeJesus still announces the calls; D reverses the logic, since it is ABS that makes rule changes easy, not the players who demand them.
  • 39. Morgan Sword says the introduction of ABS… → D (an attempt to ensure baseball retained a young audience). ‘ABS was part of a larger project to make baseball more exciting since executives are terrified of losing younger fans, as has been the case with horse racing and boxing.’ A misuses the word ‘experiment’ (Sword runs the experiment, but he never doubts the outcome); B is the wrong paragraph — other sports appear in paragraph 8, in the writer’s voice, not Sword’s; C confuses attitudes about the role of sport with fans’ taste in matches.
  • 40. Why does the writer include the views of Noe and Russo? → C (to indicate that accuracy is not the same thing as enjoyment). Noe attacks ‘algorithms [that] take the hard choices of living out of life’; Russo says ‘perfect would be weird… What is there to talk about?’ The writer places them together and adds his own line: ‘We watch baseball to kill time, not to maximize it.’ A fails because both men take the same side; B is too broad and is Russo’s point about life, not about sport; D is about audience numbers, which belongs to Sword.

Technique: in purpose questions, ask ‘what would be lost if this paragraph were deleted?’ Delete Noe and Russo and you lose the counter-argument to accuracy — so C.

Skill 4 — Matching features: who says what

This passage has five named voices. Exam papers often turn that into a matching task, and even when they do not, a who-is-who list stops you attributing an opinion to the wrong person (the Q39 trap).

  • Fred DeJesus — the umpire who used ABS first. He is described, but he expresses no opinion at all. Any option quoting his view would be wrong.
  • Rob Manfred (Commissioner) — ABS ‘will reduce controversy in the game, and be good for the game’. The pro-technology position.
  • Frank Viola (coach, ex-major-league pitcher) — it works as designed but is unforgiving, pedantic, legalistic; skill used to be rewarded and there was a dialogue with the umpire.
  • Morgan Sword (baseball operations) — explains motive (young fans, more action) and flexibility (any zone can be loaded in). Practical, not philosophical.
  • Alva Noe (professor) — the strongest opposition: a ‘terrible idea’, part of a movement to use algorithms ‘to take the hard choices of living out of life’.
  • Joe Russo (player) — accuracy is not the answer; ‘I think perfect would be weird’.

Test yourself: who thinks the technology is accurate but still a problem? Viola and Russo. Who never questions accuracy? Manfred and Sword. Sorting speakers by what they concede, not just what they attack, is what matching-features questions reward.

Skill 5 — Locating information fast

Which paragraph would you scan for each idea? Train yourself to jump to the right block instead of re-reading from the top.

  • How the machine physically looked and where it sat → paragraph 1.
  • The definition and measurements of the strike zone → paragraph 2 (in brackets).
  • What spectators are and are not allowed to do to an umpire → paragraph 3.
  • The adjustment made after the first season → paragraph 4.
  • A criticism from someone who used to pitch → paragraph 5.
  • Why MLB started the project at all → paragraph 6.
  • Ideas that were considered and rejected → paragraph 7.
  • Technology used in other sports → paragraph 8.
  • Opinions from outside the sport → paragraphs 9 and 10.

Tip: in an interview-based article, write the speaker’s surname beside each paragraph as you skim. Names are the cheapest index this kind of passage offers — and Q38–40 are all indexed by either a number or a name.

The passage adds ‘a type of play lacking much action’. That is why Q30 is NO: the fastball made the game duller, not more exciting.
Q4
B. subjective assessment
A judgment call is a decision based on personal opinion — a subjective assessment (Q34, option D on the exam paper).
Q5
C. Because saying nothing does not prove that people agreed.
‘No heckling… Nobody said a word’ describes silence only. Turning silence into approval is the classic distractor move — the answer is G, total silence.

He says it works as designed but is ‘unforgiving and pedantic’; umpires once rewarded a pitch thrown where you aimed ‘even if it was an inch or two outside’. This supports Q38 = B.

‘Dialogue’ is figurative: a human relationship in which skill was recognised. Its loss is what the writer suggests reduces the appeal of the game.

‘Executives are terrified of losing younger fans, as has been the case with horse racing and boxing.’ Option A comes from paragraph 8, which is the writer speaking, not Sword (Q39 = D on the exam paper).
