Pulling Strings to Build Pyramids · Practice Workbook

📋 Passage Overview

This passage explores an unusual theory about how the Egyptian pyramids might have been built. Californian software consultant Maureen Clemmons noticed a strange hieroglyph and wondered whether ancient Egyptians used giant kites, rather than only human labour, to lift heavy stone blocks. Aeronautics professor Morteza Gharib and his student Emilio Graff tested the idea with a real experiment, successfully lifting a multi-tonne stone column using wind power alone.

The passage weighs this evidence — the surprising experimental success, supporting clues about Egyptian sailing and pulley skills — against sceptical voices who say there is simply no proof kites were ever actually used on the pyramids themselves.

💡 Reading strategy tip: This passage moves chronologically through an experiment: hypothesis → first calculations → first real test → surprising result → expert reactions → wider evidence → future applications. Tracking this timeline helps you locate True/False/Not Given answers quickly.

🔍 Complex Sentence Analysis

"While perusing a book on the monuments of Egypt, she noticed a hieroglyph that showed a row of men standing in odd postures."
Main Clauseshe noticed a hieroglyph
Subordinate Clause"While perusing a book..." (reduced time clause, = "while she was perusing..."); "that showed a row of men..." (relative clause modifying "hieroglyph")
Grammar NoteThe participle clause "while perusing" drops the subject and auxiliary verb because it shares the same subject as the main clause — a common way to compress two actions into one sentence
Simple MeaningAs she read a book about Egyptian monuments, Clemmons spotted an unusual symbol showing men in strange positions.
中文在浏览一本关于埃及古迹的书时，她注意到一个象形文字，画着一排姿势古怪的男子。
"Their initial calculations and scale-model wind-tunnel experiments convinced them they wouldn't need a strong wind to lift the 33.5-tonne column."
Main ClauseTheir initial calculations and...experiments convinced them...
Subordinate Clause"(that) they wouldn't need a strong wind to lift the column" (noun clause, object of "convinced")
Grammar Note"convince someone (that)..." takes a noun clause as its object; the conjunction "that" is omitted, which is common in informal/journalistic writing
Simple MeaningEarly tests showed the researchers that even a weak wind could lift the heavy column.
中文他们最初的计算和小型风洞实验使他们确信，不需要强风就能吊起这根重33.5吨的石柱。
"This jerk meant that kites could lift huge weights, Gharib realised."
Main ClauseThis jerk meant that kites could lift huge weights / Gharib realised (this)
Subordinate Clause"that kites could lift huge weights" (noun clause, object of "meant")
Grammar Note"Gharib realised" is an inserted reporting clause placed after the main statement — common in journalistic style to attribute an idea without breaking the flow
Simple MeaningGharib understood that the sudden jerking force showed kites were capable of lifting very heavy objects.
中文葛里布意识到，这种猛拉的力量意味着风筝能够吊起巨大的重物。
"Although it dates from several hundred years after the building of the pyramids, its sophistication suggests that the Egyptians might have been developing ideas of flight for a long time."
Main Clauseits sophistication suggests that the Egyptians might have been developing ideas of flight...
Subordinate Clause"Although it dates from..." (concessive clause); "that the Egyptians might have been developing..." (noun clause, object of "suggests")
Grammar Note"Although" introduces a concession that seems to weaken the claim (the artefact is too recent), but the main clause still asserts the claim is possible — a common hedge-and-argue structure in academic writing
Simple MeaningEven though the glider-like object is from a later period, its advanced design hints the Egyptians explored flight ideas for a long time.
中文尽管它的年代比金字塔建造晚了几百年，但其精密程度表明，埃及人可能长期以来一直在研究飞行方面的构想。
"So whether they were actually used to build the pyramids or not, it seems that kites may make sensible construction tools in the 21st century AD."
Main Clauseit seems that kites may make sensible construction tools in the 21st century AD
Subordinate Clause"whether they were actually used...or not" (concessive/conditional noun clause); "that kites may make sensible..." (noun clause, subject complement of "seems")
Grammar Note"whether...or not" expresses that the outcome doesn't affect the following conclusion — regardless of the historical answer, the modern application still stands
Simple MeaningWhether or not kites really helped build the pyramids, they could still be a practical building tool today.
中文因此，无论风筝是否真的曾被用来建造金字塔，它们在21世纪似乎都可能成为实用的建筑工具。

🧠 Reading Logic Training

Cause & Effect

Cause: the kite opened suddenly into the wind, creating a huge initial "jerk" force five times larger than the steady wind force. → Effect: the 33.5-tonne column was pulled upright in just 40 seconds, far faster and more powerfully than the team's calculations had predicted.

Contrast

The passage contrasts the team's excitement and success ("We were absolutely stunned") with the scepticism of other Egyptologists, such as Willeke Wendrich, who insists "the evidence for kite-lifting is non-existent."

Comparison

The "kite theory" is compared with the "brute force method" (many labourers dragging stones on sledges) — Gharib states plainly that the evidence for each theory is equally weak, neither being proven better than the other.

Examples

The passage gives concrete supporting examples for ancient flight/wind knowledge: the glider-like wooden artefact from Saqqara, Egyptian wooden pulleys, and Chinese kites used since 1250 BC for messages and warfare.

Definition

A "hieroglyph" is implicitly explained as a picture-symbol from ancient Egyptian writing — in this case, one showing men holding ropes connected to what might be a giant kite rather than a real bird.

Problem / Solution

Problem: there is no direct pictorial evidence showing exactly how the pyramids were built. Response: rather than solving the historical mystery, researchers instead test whether the kite method is physically possible, which shifts the debate from certainty to plausibility.

Chronology

The passage follows a clear timeline: Clemmons's book discovery → contacting Gharib → initial calculations and small-scale wind-tunnel tests → the full-scale experiment earlier "this year" → expert reactions → a modern-day application proposal in Nicaragua.

Author's Attitude

The writer stays balanced and cautious throughout — presenting the experiment's success enthusiastically, but also including direct sceptical quotes, and ending with a careful, non-committal conclusion: kites "may make sensible construction tools," not that they definitely built the pyramids.
