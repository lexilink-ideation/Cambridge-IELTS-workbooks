Reading Passage 3

Cambridge IELTS 20 — Test 1 — Reading Passage 3

How Stress Affects Our Judgement · MCQ + Sentence Endings + Yes / No / Not Given (Q27–40)

📋 Passage Overview

A neuroscientist describes her own research into how stress changes the way we process information. Working with firefighters, whose shifts swing between calm and life-threatening, and then with students facing a surprise public speech, she found that stress makes people much better at taking in bad news — even bad news that has nothing to do with them — while leaving their response to good news unchanged. The second half explains the evolutionary reason for this ‘switch’, shows how stress spreads from person to person and through social media, and ends on a hopeful note: positive emotions are contagious too.

What the passage argues: That stress does not simply make us think worse — it makes us selectively better at absorbing threatening information, an ancient survival mechanism that is now switched on by emails, news alerts and social media, and that can produce collective fear which is not always justified.

段落结构 Structure: 第1段：引入问题——压力下我们处理信息的能力是变强还是变弱？第2段：为何选择消防员（工作时松时紧）。第3段：核心发现（压力只让「坏消息」更易被吸收）。第4段：研究方法（估计40种负面事件的概率 → 给好/坏消息 → 重新估计）。第5段：放松时乐观、压力下对坏消息高度警觉。第6段：学生演讲实验重复了同一模式。第7段：大脑「开关」与神经信号。第8段：进化解释与抑郁焦虑。第9–10段：压力在人与人、社交媒体间传播。第11段：集体恐慌的循环。第12段：希望同样具有传染性。

Task type: Q27–30 Multiple choice (each tied to a numbered paragraph — count the paragraphs before you start); Q31–35 Sentence endings A–G (seven endings, five gaps, so two are never used); Q36–40 YES / NO / NOT GIVEN about the writer’s claims.

💡 Reading strategy tip: Passage 3 is written in the first person by the researcher herself, so the questions test whether you can tell apart four different kinds of sentence: her purpose (why she studied firefighters), her method (what she asked people to do), her results (what she found), and her interpretation (what it means). Q29 asks for exactly this distinction. For the sentence-ending task (Q31–35), read the unfinished sentence first and predict the grammar of the ending — four of the seven endings start with a past-tense verb, so the subject of your ending must fit. For YES / NO / NOT GIVEN, remember you are judging the writer’s claims, not facts about the world.
⚠️ Traps in this section 本节陷阱:
  • Q29 — results vs method. Paragraph 3 gives the finding; paragraph 4 begins ‘This is how we arrived at these results’ and then lists what the researchers did. That is methodology, not an explanation of findings.
  • Count the paragraphs carefully. The MCQs name the second, fourth and seventh paragraphs. The seventh begins ‘When we experience stressful events…’ — if you miscount you will answer from the evolution paragraph instead and choose the wrong option.
  • Q33 ‘always’. The sentence is about good news, and the passage says stress didn’t change how they responded to it. The correct ending is F (‘behaved in a similar manner, regardless of the circumstances’), not C, which describes treating good and bad news the same way — the opposite of the finding.
  • Q37 is a comparison the writer never makes. Phones are linked to stress by an APA survey, but no other device is mentioned at all, so there is nothing to compare — NOT GIVEN.
  • Q38 reverses the passage. Stress enhances the likelihood that people take in negative reports; the statement says we become less able to take it in — a direct contradiction, so NO.
  • Two endings are unused in Q31–35 (A and C). Do not force every letter into an answer, and do not panic when two look attractive — check the grammar of the unfinished sentence first.

🔍 Complex Sentence Analysis

Academic Reading sentences are long because they stack clauses, not because the words are hard. Find the main verb first, then peel off the extra layers. 先找主句谓语动词，再逐层剥离修饰成分。
“These ups and downs presented the perfect setting for an experiment on how people’s ability to use information changes when they feel under pressure.”
Main ClauseThese ups and downs presented the perfect setting for an experiment.
Subordinate / Relative‘on how people’s ability… changes’ = an embedded question after a preposition; ‘when they feel under pressure’ = a time clause inside it.
Grammar NoteAfter a preposition, a question becomes statement word order (how ability changes, not how does ability change). ‘These ups and downs’ refers back to the whole previous description — a summary noun phrase.
Simple MeaningBecause firefighters’ days switch between calm and dangerous, they were ideal subjects for a study of decision-making under pressure.
中文这种忙闲交替为研究「人在压力下运用信息的能力会如何变化」提供了理想的实验场景。
“We found that perceived threat acted as a trigger for a stress reaction that made the task of processing information easier for the firefighters — but only as long as it conveyed bad news.”
Main ClauseWe found that perceived threat acted as a trigger for a stress reaction.
Subordinate / Relative‘that made the task… easier’ = a defining relative clause describing the reaction; ‘but only as long as it conveyed bad news’ = a condition attached at the end.
Grammar NoteThe dash introduces the limit on the finding. In academic writing the sentence after a dash usually narrows the claim — and that narrowing is what the questions test.
Simple MeaningFeeling threatened started a stress response that helped firefighters absorb information — but only if the information was bad.
中文我们发现，感知到的威胁会触发一种压力反应，使消防员更容易处理信息——但前提是这些信息传达的是坏消息。
“Under these conditions, they became hyper-vigilant to bad news, even when it had nothing to do with their job, and altered their beliefs in response.”
Main ClauseThey became hyper-vigilant to bad news… and altered their beliefs in response.
Subordinate / Relative‘even when it had nothing to do with their job’ = a concessive time clause dropped between the two verbs.
Grammar NoteTwo main verbs (became, altered) share one subject, but a clause has been inserted between them. Even when = this was true in the surprising case too.
Simple MeaningWhen stressed, they noticed bad news much more — even unrelated bad news — and changed what they believed.
中文在这种情况下，他们对坏消息变得高度警觉，即使那些消息与其工作毫无关系，并据此改变了自己的看法。
“Brain imaging reveals that this ‘switch’ is related to a sudden boost in a neural signal important for learning, specifically in response to unexpected warning signs, such as faces expressing fear.”
Main ClauseBrain imaging reveals that this ‘switch’ is related to a sudden boost in a neural signal.
Subordinate / Relative‘important for learning’ = a reduced relative (which is important for learning); ‘specifically in response to…’ + ‘such as…’ = narrowing and exemplifying phrases.
Grammar NoteNotice how the sentence gets more precise as it goes on: signal → important for learning → in response to warning signs → e.g. fearful faces. This is the sentence Q30 is built on.
Simple MeaningScans show the switch involves a sudden rise in a learning signal in the brain, especially when we see surprising signs of danger such as frightened faces.
中文脑成像显示，这一「开关」与一种对学习至关重要的神经信号的骤然增强有关，尤其是在面对意外危险信号（如流露恐惧的面孔）时。
“In other words, a pre-programmed physiological reaction, which evolution has equipped us with to help us avoid famished predators, is now being triggered by an online post.”
Main ClauseA pre-programmed physiological reaction is now being triggered by an online post.
Subordinate / Relative‘which evolution has equipped us with to help us avoid famished predators’ = a non-defining relative clause containing an infinitive of purpose.
Grammar NoteThe relative clause separates the subject from its verb by fourteen words. Find reaction… is being triggered first, then read the middle. ‘Equip somebody with something’ keeps its preposition at the end of the clause.
Simple MeaningA survival response evolution gave us for escaping hungry predators is now set off by a social media post.
中文换句话说，进化赋予我们用以躲避饥饿捕食者的那种预设生理反应，如今却被一条网络帖子所触发。
“The good news, however, is that positive emotions, such as hope, are contagious too, and are powerful in inducing people to act to find solutions.”
Main ClauseThe good news is that positive emotions are contagious too and are powerful in inducing people to act.
Subordinate / Relative‘such as hope’ = an example inside the subject of the that-clause; ‘to find solutions’ = an infinitive of purpose.
Grammar NoteThe good news is that… is a discourse marker signalling the writer’s turn from warning to encouragement. This final paragraph is where Q40 (YES) is answered.
Simple MeaningHappily, feelings like hope spread between people as well, and they make people want to look for solutions.
中文不过好消息是，希望等积极情绪同样具有传染性，而且能有力地促使人们采取行动去寻找解决方案。

🧠 Reading Logic Training

IELTS Reading tests whether you follow the relationship between ideas: cause, contrast, example, definition, sequence and the writer’s own attitude. 抓住句子之间的逻辑关系，比逐词翻译更重要。

Cause & effect — the loop that creates collective fear

Paragraph 11 is one long causal circle: a stressful public event → a wave of alarming media → stress is triggered → stress spreads person to person → people absorb more negative reports → stress increases further → trips are cancelled and stocks are sold. The writer calls it ‘a reliable pattern’. Q39 is answered inside this loop.

Contrast — good news versus bad news

The finding only makes sense as a contrast: stress made bad news easier to process, but “In contrast, stress didn’t change how they responded to good news.” Q33 depends entirely on that sentence — the response to good news stayed the same, which is ending F, not ending C.

Comparison — where the passage does and does not compare

The passage compares relaxed days with hectic days, firefighters with students, and prehistoric humans with modern phone users. It never compares phones with other devices, which is why Q37 is NOT GIVEN. Before answering a comparison question, ask: are both things actually in the text?

Examples — ‘such as’ carries most of the concrete detail

“adverse events… such as being involved in an accident or becoming a victim of card fraud”; “warning signs, such as faces expressing fear”; “positive feeds… such as images of a pink sunset”. Card fraud is the most important example in the passage because it is unrelated to firefighting — that is what makes the result surprising.

Definition & restatement — ‘In other words’

“In other words, a pre-programmed physiological reaction… is now being triggered by an online post.” When a writer restates, the second version is usually simpler and is the one the questions paraphrase. Also note the self-definition of ‘this switch’ in paragraph 7.

Problem → explanation → solution

Problem: stress makes us absorb alarming information and spreads fear. Explanation: an evolutionary switch that once protected us from predators. Solution (final paragraph): because hope is contagious too, we can ‘frame our messages more effectively’. Q40 sits in the solution stage — and the writer clearly approves of it.

Method vs findings — the distinction Q29 is built on

Paragraph 3 = findings (‘We found that…’). Paragraph 4 = method (‘We asked… We then gave them… and asked them to provide new estimates’). Paragraph 5 = results in detail. Learn the verbs: asked, gave, measured, recorded signal method; found, revealed, showed signal results.

The writer’s attitude — concerned, but not pessimistic

She warns that collective fear is ‘not always justified’ and that reactions built for ‘famished predators’ now fire at emails. But the last paragraph begins ‘The good news, however…’ and ends with ‘conscientious agents of change’. In YES/NO/NOT GIVEN, any statement claiming she thinks the situation is hopeless would be NO.

🎯 Question-Type Skill Practice

Skill 1 — Multiple choice, paragraph by paragraph (Q27–30)

Each question names a paragraph, so locate it first and read the whole paragraph before looking at the options. Then eliminate.

  • 27. How the writer introduces the topic → C — ‘we are all sometimes required to weigh up information under stressful conditions’: a challenge everyone faces. B is wrong because no widely held assumption is stated and then questioned; D is wrong because no single most-anxious situation is specified.
  • 28. The point about firefighters → A — their days swing between relaxed and hectic, and ‘these ups and downs presented the perfect setting for an experiment’. B and D describe the firefighters themselves, which the paragraph never does.
  • 29. What the writer is doing in paragraph 4 → D — ‘We asked… We then gave them… and asked them to provide new estimates’ is a description of methodology. The findings were already given in the previous paragraph.
  • 30. The brain mechanism in paragraph 7 → C — a boost in a learning signal ‘specifically in response to unexpected warning signs’ = heightened sensitivity to external threats. A (speed) and B (controlling anxiety) are never claimed.

Method: for ‘what is the writer doing’ questions, ignore the content and label the paragraph’s function in one word before you read the options: aim, method, result, explanation, example.

Skill 2 — Matching sentence endings (Q31–35)

Seven endings, five questions: two are never used. Read the stem, predict the meaning, then find the ending that also fits grammatically.

  • 31. When relaxed, the firefighters usually… → B (took relatively little notice of bad news) — ‘they will ignore bad news and embrace the good… This is what happened when the firefighters were relaxed.’
  • 32. When stressed, they… → G (thought it more likely that they would experience something bad) — they became hyper-vigilant to bad news and ‘altered their beliefs in response’.
  • 33. Told good news, they always… → F (behaved in a similar manner, regardless of the circumstances) — ‘stress didn’t change how they responded to good news’.
  • 34. The students’ cortisol levels rose when the researchers… → E (put them in a stressful situation) — they were told they had to give a surprise public speech that would be judged and posted online.
  • 35. Negative information was processed better when subjects… → D (were feeling under stress) — the finding common to both experiments.

Unused: A (made them feel optimistic) and C (responded to negative and positive information in the same way). C is the classic near-miss for Q33 — it says the two kinds of news were treated alike, whereas the passage says only the good-news response was unchanged.

Skill 3 — YES / NO / NOT GIVEN (Q36–40)

You are judging the writer’s claims. NO means she claims the opposite; NOT GIVEN means she never takes a position.

  • 36. Our posts reflect the tone of our feeds. → YES — positive feeds → uplifting posts; negative posts → ‘we will in turn create more negative posts’.
  • 37. Phones affect stress more than other devices. → NOT GIVEN — the APA survey links phone-checking to stress, but no other device is mentioned, so no comparison exists.
  • 38. The more we read about a stressful event, the less we take in. → NO — the writer claims the opposite: stress ‘temporarily enhances the likelihood that people will take in negative reports’.
  • 39. Social-media stress can lead to unnecessary precautions. → YES — ‘trips are cancelled, even if the disaster took place across the globe; stocks are sold, even when holding on is the best thing to do’.
  • 40. Being affected by others’ moods can be used positively. → YES — hope is contagious too and is ‘powerful in inducing people to act to find solutions’.

Habit: for every NO you choose, be able to point at the sentence that says the opposite. If you cannot, the answer is probably NOT GIVEN.

Skill 4 — Locating information quickly

Which paragraph would you scan for each idea? In a 12-paragraph passage, speed comes from knowing the shape of the argument.

  • Why firefighters were chosen → paragraph 2.
  • The one-sentence summary of the result → paragraph 3.
  • What participants were actually asked to do → paragraph 4.
  • The student speech experiment → paragraph 6.
  • Brain imaging and the neural ‘switch’ → paragraph 7.
  • Depression and anxiety → paragraph 8.
  • Emotions spreading through social media → paragraph 9.
  • Cancelled trips and sold stocks → paragraph 11.

Tip: mark the paragraph numbers in the margin as you skim. Three of the four MCQs here refer to paragraphs by number, so a miscount costs you marks directly.

Skill 5 — Summary completion practice (extra)

Same passage, different task type. Complete each sentence with NO MORE THAN TWO WORDS from the passage, then check.

  • The firefighters estimated their chance of experiencing ______ different adverse events. → 40
  • The stressed students became better at processing information that was ______ but alarming. → unrelated
  • Evolution gave us this reaction to help us avoid ______. → famished predators
  • Constant vigilance in a safe environment would have been ______. → wasteful
  • According to one study, social media posting enlarges your ______. → pupils
  • The writer hopes readers will become conscientious ______. → agents of change

Skill 6 — Matching headings practice (extra)

Give each part of the passage a heading. A good heading names the paragraph’s job, not its most colourful detail.

  • Paragraphs 1–2 → A question, and an unusual place to answer it.
  • Paragraphs 3–5 → How the study worked, and what it showed.
  • Paragraph 6 → The same result outside the fire station.
  • Paragraphs 7–8 → A switch in the brain, and why we have it — not ‘The problem of depression’, which is only the closing detail.
  • Paragraphs 9–10 → How stress passes between people.
  • Paragraphs 11–12 → From collective fear to contagious hope.

Watch out: paragraphs 7 and 8 both discuss the brain. Paragraph 7 describes the mechanism; paragraph 8 explains its evolutionary purpose. Headings must separate what from why.

Review Quiz

Q1
C. by mentioning a difficulty that everybody faces
‘We are all sometimes required to weigh up information under stressful conditions.’ The paragraph ends with an open question, which is not the same as questioning an assumption.
Q2
A. Their stress levels change regularly during their working lives.
Some days are ‘pretty relaxed’, others ‘hectic’: ‘These ups and downs presented the perfect setting for an experiment.’
Q3
D. describes the methodology
It opens ‘This is how we arrived at these results’ and then lists the procedure: estimate → receive good or bad news → estimate again. The findings came in the paragraph before.
Q4
B. It applied only when the information was bad news.
‘…made the task of processing information easier — but only as long as it conveyed bad news.’ C is the opposite: the effect worked even for unrelated bad news such as card fraud.
Q5
B. They pay little attention to bad news.
‘People are normally quite optimistic — they will ignore bad news and embrace the good.’ This is ending B in the sentence-completion task.
Q6
C. It has nothing to do with firefighting, yet stressed subjects still absorbed it.
The stress effect worked on bad news ‘even when it had nothing to do with their job’ — that is what makes the finding surprising.
Q7
B. behaved in a similar manner, regardless of the circumstances
‘Stress didn’t change how they responded to good news’ — their behaviour was the same whether relaxed or stressed. Option A is the trap: it describes treating the two kinds of news alike, which the study disproves.
Q8
C. It increases sensitivity to signs of danger from outside.
‘A sudden boost in a neural signal important for learning, specifically in response to unexpected warning signs, such as faces expressing fear.’
Q9
A. Constant high alert would be wasteful in a safe environment.
‘In a safe environment, however, it would have been wasteful to be on high alert constantly.’ The passage adds that people with depression and anxiety seem unable to switch away.
Q10
B. Repeatedly checking your phone is linked to stress.
The APA survey links repeated checking with stress. Because no other device is discussed, the comparison in Q37 is NOT GIVEN.
Q11
C. Trips are cancelled although the disaster happened on the other side of the world.
The passage also gives ‘stocks are sold, even when holding on is the best thing to do’. Both are precautions the situation does not justify.
Q12
C. Emotional contagion also works for hope, so messages can be framed to help.
‘Positive emotions, such as hope, are contagious too, and are powerful in inducing people to act to find solutions… become conscientious agents of change.’
